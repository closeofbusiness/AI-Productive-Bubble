The unit that crashed was the breeding organism (dollars per ram, per tree, per fancy fowl, per bulb). Wool, raw silk, eggs, and cut flowers were the output, and their prices were set by tariffs, war, and imports. Almost none of the written-down organisms became a cheap input to a different industry. PART B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: Four biological stocks, four metered inputs. None was a durable network, so “idle capacity” means animals killed, trees burned or blighted, birds eaten, or contracts unwound.

Merino. About 20,000 head were imported to the United States in 1810–11 (Woods’s estimate for 1810–11; Fuller: Spain exported 20,000 in 1810). Jarvis moved nearly 4,000. By 1840 New England had about 4 million sheep, of which about 70 percent, roughly 2.8 million head, were merinos or grades. Vermont peaked at 1,681,819 sheep in 1840, clipping 3,699,235 pounds, about 2.2 pounds per head. The metered speculative input was dollars per ram. In 1810–11 rams commonly brought $300 to $500, and as much as $1,000; one 1810 purchase was $6,000 for four rams ($1,500 per ram). Humphreys sold two pairs for $6,000. There was no prospectus. Buyers were paying the scarcity price of Spanish blood. The wool output, a different series, went from $1.50 per pound at the end of the War of 1812 to $0.40 per pound, then from 36 cents per pound in 1827 (39 cents in the 1837 statistical view) to 57 cents in 1835, then to 25 cents in the late 1840s. A Vermont profit sheet for 1837 used 50.5 cents per pound. Keeping a sheep cost $1 to $2 per head per year in New England against about 25 cents in the West, so 25-cent wool on a 2.2-pound fleece (about 55 cents of wool per sheep per year) sat below the northeastern cost. After 1815, surplus merinos were killed for mutton. Vermont flocks then fell from 1,681,819 sheep in 1840 to 1,014,122 in 1850 and 297,000 in 1900. In 1867–70 some Vermont sheep sold at 75 cents to $1 per head. The best rams were not permanently cheap: later stud rams brought as much as $3,500.

Hen fever. The stock was fancy fowl, concentrated at shows: not quite 1,600 birds at Boston in 1849, and by one account nearly 12,000 entries in the same tent in 1860, about half of them crosses and grades. The metered input was dollars per bird or per egg: about $1 per egg and up to $120 per pair at the peak. By 1855 prices barely covered freight from Asia, and owners could not give the birds away. Burnham’s disposal was to eat them. No census gives a national flock created by the fever. P* was the exhibition price, not a meat or egg cost of production.

Morus multicaulis. The metered input was dollars per tree, and also per bud, joint, or inch. Nurseries had sold at $4 per hundred in 1834, $10 in 1835, and $30 in 1836. Early 1839: $1 to $1.25 per tree. End of 1839: 2 to 4 cents per tree, about 2 to 4 percent of the early-1839 price. Ohio sales in 1838 were quoted in the tens of cents per tree; in 1842 a buyer was offered trees at $5 per thousand. Cheney Brothers at one time held 100,000 trees and in 1838 expected a 700 percent return on European purchases. The Silk Culturist listed 2 million white mulberries and 320,000 multicaulis for sale. Prince imported more than 1 million trees from France in 1839. An auction of 30,000 trees found no buyers. Trees were burned, composted, or sold as pea brush. Winter 1840 and the 1844 blight removed what planting did not. P*, as a nurseryman wrote in June 1836, was any price above the cost of growing them; below that, the tree trade had no point. Silkworms were the excuse. Cocoons in one Ohio offer were 25 to 50 cents per pound, or $2 to $4 per bushel.

Tulips. The metered input was guilders per bulb, and for the tavern futures, guilders per aas (1 aas = 1/20 gram). A Semper Augustus bulb brought 2,000 guilders in 1625. Common Switser bulbs went from 0.035 guilders per aas on 23 January 1637 to 0.17 on 5 February, then 0.11 on 6 and 9 February. Goldgar’s dearest receipt was 5,000 guilders, about the price of a good house; she found 37 people who paid more than 300 guilders, roughly a craftsman’s annual earnings. Rare bulbs then fell about 32 percent a year from February 1637 to 1642, against about 28.5 percent a year for prized eighteenth-century bulbs, so Garber assigns at most about 16 percent of that drop to the crash itself. By 1739 the old prize bulbs were at or under 0.1 guilder. Thompson’s reading is that the famous “prices” were option exercise prices after futures were converted into options, not cleared cash prices above fundamental value. Goldgar found no effect on production, so the gardens did not sit idle as stranded plant.
SOURCE: https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdf/10.1257/jep.4.2.35

BENEFICIARY: Western and national commercial wool sheep (Ohio Valley from the 1830s; range flocks after the Civil War)
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Merino grading from the 1810s; Ohio competition visible once the Pennsylvania and Ohio canals opened, 1832–34; Vermont breeders shipping blood west by the carload in the 1870s
CHEAP INPUT: Merino blood after the 1810–11 imports, once rams were no longer $300 to $1,000 each. The binding cheap input for western growers was land and keep, about 25 cents per sheep per year, against $1 to $2 in New England. Wool at 25 cents per pound in the late 1840s is the output price that ruined Vermont, not a subsidy invented for the West.
HOW USED: Growers graded ordinary flocks toward merino wool and sold that wool to the same eastern mills the New England flocks had supplied. By 1840 about 2.8 million of New England’s 4 million sheep were already merinos or grades. This is more wool sheep for wool buyers.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Cheap pasture and, later, the canals and railroads. Those stayed valuable. The genetic premium on ordinary grades was competed away; the premium on named Vermont pedigrees was not.
LAG: About 20 years from the 1810–11 imports to canal-borne western wool; about 60 years to the post-1872 range buildup.
FORESEEN?: The importers of 1810 wanted American fine wool. They did not describe Ohio and the Far West taking the clip away from Vermont.

BENEFICIARY: New England woolen mills, from the Hartford Woolen Manufactory (1788) and the Scholfields’ carding works (1790s) through the Providence Woolen Manufacturing Company (1812) and Vermont’s small mill flock
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Mills from 1788; a wartime wave in 1807–15; Vermont from 33 woolen mills in 1836 to about 100 by 1850
CHEAP INPUT: Fine wool, dollars per pound. It was scarce and dear during the embargo (Gallatin in 1810 still called the wool supply deficient in quality and quantity). It fell from $1.50 per pound to $0.40 at the peace of 1815, recovered to 57 cents in 1835, and was 25 cents in the late 1840s. Mills were the customers the sheep were imported to serve. Ram prices were not their input.
HOW USED: They carded, spun, fulled, and finished cloth for the domestic market the embargo had closed to British broadcloth. A student count of 334 Vermont factories in 1837 is rejected; the National Register count is about 100 mills by 1850, and Woods has more than 150 by mid-century. Average plants were small. Several wartime mills, including Providence, shut when British cloth returned.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Waterpower, skilled finishers, and the tariff. The tariff, not a pile of cheap merinos, kept the branch alive after 1815. When protection fell, the gain went to cloth buyers and to mills that could use western wool. Vermont mill villages lost the local clip.
LAG: Zero from the sheep build-out. The mills were simultaneous with the mania, and several died at the 1815 price break.
FORESEEN?: Yes. Hamilton in 1791, the embargos, and the importers themselves treated domestic cloth as the point of better sheep.

BENEFICIARY: Cheney Brothers and the American silk-manufacturing industry (Mt. Nebo mill, South Manchester, Connecticut; the national throwing and weaving trade that followed)
TYPE: UNCLEAR (a real industry, and a false third claim: the mill ran on imported raw silk after the trees were worthless)
DATES: Partnership agreed November 1837; mill raised 1838 with $50,000 of capital, in a building 32 by 45 feet. Sewing silk in 1844 at $6.50 to $7.50 per pound. Machine-twist for sewing machines begun 1852. Waste-silk spinning 1855. Capital $400,000 by 1855. About 600 workers and roughly $551,000 of annual production by 1860. US silk manufacturing, in the firm’s own 1916 history, from $6,600,000 in 1860 to $197,000,000 in 1910, and from 5,000 employees to 120,000. US mills consumed 10,700,000 kilograms of silk in 1913, as much as France, Germany, Italy, and England combined.
CHEAP INPUT: Raw silk imported from Asia, dollars per pound of reeled silk, not mulberry trees. Domestic reeling was already too uneven for mills in the 1820s, when the United States was importing more than $7 million of silk goods (1824). Trees fell from $1–$1.25 to $0.02–$0.04 each during 1839 and were burned. Cheney sewing silk was made almost entirely from Asian silk.
HOW USED: They threw, dyed, and wove imported fiber into sewing silk, ribbon, and later dress goods, and from 1855 spun pierced and tangled cocoons that reelers had treated as waste. The Civil War tariff on silk goods, with raw silk left comparatively free, is what the company history credits for the weaving boom. The Mansfield silk mill, planted in the old cocoon district, failed with the multicaulis speculation. The Hanks thread shed of 1810 had already been abandoned in 1828.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Family money (the artist brothers John and Seth carried the firm), the Rixford roller and Frank Cheney’s 1847 thread machine, and the tariff. Those held value for decades. The trees did not. A 1923 “profit” of $23 million on the Connecticut Humanities page is out of scale with the 1910 industry total and is treated as unreliable until an annual report is read.
LAG: The mill was started during the boom, a year before the October 1839 break. The takeoff in thread and waste silk was about 15 years after the crash.
FORESEEN?: The brothers chose manufacture in 1837, before the crash was complete. The sewing-machine thread trade (1852) and the later national scale were not in the mulberry manuals.
SOURCE: https://history.textiles.ncsu.edu/textile-companies/cheney-brothers-silk/
QUOTE: "The first products of the factory were sewing silk, which was made almost entirely with silk imported from Asia."

BENEFICIARY: Vermont dairy (home cheese and butter, then cheese factories, creameries, and fluid milk)
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: Cows were being sold to make room for sheep in the 1830s. Butter and cheese became the fallback in the late 1840s. Commercial scale from 1850. First Vermont cheese factory, Consider Bardwell at Pawlet, 1864. Cheese output peaked at 4,830,700 pounds in 1869. North River Creamery, Jacksonville, 1886. About 300 butter factories in 1915, producing 20,423,529 pounds of butter that year. Franklin County creamery at St. Albans made 25,000 pounds of butter a day in the 1890s. Fluid milk from Bellows Falls to Boston in 1890.
CHEAP INPUT: The written-down asset was the hill farm, not the sheep. Wool at 25 cents per pound in the late 1840s, against a keep of $1 to $2 per sheep per year, made the pasture worth more in cows. Dairy cows rose from 146,128 in 1850 to 270,194 in 1900, an 85 percent increase, while sheep fell from about 1.7 million in 1840 to 297,000 in 1900.
HOW USED: Farmers sold butter, cheese, and later fluid milk to Boston, New York, and Montreal, a different customer from the woolen mills. The National Register states that the sheep decline of the late 1840s forced the search for another use, and that the railroad plus the iced butter car of 1854 made the commercial dairy possible.
MECHANISM: NEW-LOCATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Rail access, the ice car, and urban demand. Those captured the durable value. Jersey and other dairy blood became a new scarce breed, the same pattern as the merino, one species later. Consumers gained a steadier supply of butter and milk; hill farmers who could not reach a creamery left.
LAG: About 10 years from the late-1840s wool collapse to the 1850s commercial dairy; about 40 years from the 1810 sheep imports.
FORESEEN?: Not by the importers of 1810. By the 1860s, Vermont writers described butter and cheese as what people did once wool stopped paying.
SOURCE: https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/NRHP/64500686_text
QUOTE: "the decline of the sheep industry in the late 1840s forced farmers to find alternative agricultural pursuits"
UNCERTAIN: Whether dairy would have overtaken wool on these farms if wool had stayed at 57 cents per pound and the railroad had arrived anyway. The Register treats the price collapse and the railroad as jointly necessary. Farm-level accounts comparing the two margins would settle it.

BENEFICIARY: Vermont Merino stud breeders (Edmund Hammond of Middlebury; the Vermont Merino Sheep Breeders’ Association, register 1879; shipments to California, Germany, Australia, and South America)
TYPE: UNCLEAR (same-market survivor; pedigreed rams were sold dear, so this is the scarce complement collecting rent, not a user of a cheap input)
DATES: Hammond’s improvement by the 1850s; Hamburg prizes 1863; association 1876–79; “Vermont invasion” of Australian studs from the early 1880s, contested and largely reversed after 1906; last overseas shipment just before the First World War
CHEAP INPUT: None at the point of sale. Commercial culls were cheap (75 cents to $1 per head in 1867–70). Stock rams sold for as much as $3,500. Fleece yield as a share of live weight was bred from 6 percent in 1812 to 21 percent in 1865, and from 2.2 pounds per head in 1840 to 5.34 pounds in 1870. Middlebury sent 29 carloads west in 1877 and 41 carloads to the Southwest in 1879.
HOW USED: Western, German, Australian, and South American flockmasters bought rams to put more weight of wool on sheep they already owned. The buyers were sheep growers. The breeders’ own slogan was that their most profitable product was blood, not wool.
MECHANISM: COMPLEMENTARY-PRODUCT
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Registered wrinkles and yolk, and the Vermont name. It held value until scouring losses and, in Australia, the frozen-mutton trade made a heavy, greasy carcass the wrong animal. Gains stayed with the stud breeders until that fashion turned.
LAG: About 50 years from the 1815 break to the stud boom of the 1860s–70s.
FORESEEN?: No. The 1810 buyers thought they were founding a wool supply, not a late-century export stud.

BENEFICIARY: The Dutch bulb trade (Haarlem growers and later commercial floriculture)
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Luxury tulips from the late sixteenth century; the contract spike of winter 1636–37; hyacinths the fashionable bulb by 1707; eighteenth-century prize bulbs still depreciating at about 28.5 percent a year as they multiplied
CHEAP INPUT: Guilders per bulb. After February 1637 rare bulbs fell at roughly the normal reproductive rate, not to a scrap value that a new industry required. Common-bulb futures were a January–February 1637 tavern market. By 1739 the old prize names were at or under 0.1 guilder per bulb, which is what multiplication does to a rare flower over a century.
HOW USED: Growers kept selling bulbs and flowers to fanciers and, much later, to export customers. Garber’s point is that this price path is how flower bulbs behave. Goldgar found no break in production. A nineteenth-century export tonnage was not on any page fetched here.
MECHANISM: COMPLEMENTARY-PRODUCT
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The broken-tulip virus pattern, which could not be scaled from seed, and later the coastal soils and growers’ skill. The 1637 crash did not transfer those to a new class of firms. Consumers of ordinary tulips eventually got cheap bulbs because bulbs multiply.
LAG: No lag from crash to a new industry. The trade was already the industry.
FORESEEN?: Growers already knew they were in the flower business. Contemporaries read the crash as a warning about honor and credit, not as the start of an export sector.

BENEFICIARY: The American poultry fancy (Boston shows from 1849; the American Poultry Association, February 1873; the Brahma and other exhibition breeds)
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: British Cochin fashion from the early 1840s; Boston, November 1849; Burnham sent Brahmas to Queen Victoria in 1852; English standard reprinted with changes in 1867; APA at Buffalo, February 1873
CHEAP INPUT: Dollars per fancy fowl. Peak about $120 per pair and $1 per egg; by 1855 the birds would not sell and hardly covered freight from Asia. The standard that followed priced looks, not a cheap meat bird.

MECHANISM: INSTITUTIONAL
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Breed reputation and the standard book. Value stayed inside a hobby market. Eaters were not the customer.
LAG: About 18 years from the 1855 collapse to the 1873 association; about 24 years from the first Boston show.
FORESEEN?: The 1849 organizers wanted judging rules. A New York Times editorial in 1854 hoped the fever would cheapen eggs. Nobody described a meat industry.

BENEFICIARY: The US broiler industry (Cecile Steele; Arbor Acres; Vantress; the integrated firms)
TYPE: UNCLEAR (rejected as a beneficiary of hen fever; included because the draft links them)
DATES: Steele, Sussex County, Delaware, 500 meat chicks in 1923 and a 10,000-bird house by 1926. A&P Chicken of Tomorrow contest announced 1945; state contests 1946–47. Arbor Acres (Henry Saglio, Glastonbury, Connecticut) won carcass; Vantress Hatchery won feed efficiency with a Red Cornish crossed on New Hampshires. MECHANISM: OTHER (hybrid genetics, feed, and vertical integration, a century after the fancy)
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Closed breeding flocks (Arbor Acres, Cobb-Vantress, and their successors) and the integrator’s brand. Those held the profit. The exhibition standard did not.
LAG: About 68 years from 1855 to Steele; about 90 years to the Chicken of Tomorrow.
FORESEEN?: No contemporary of the hen fever described this.

BENEFICIARY: Commercial nurseries, led by Prince’s Linnaean Botanic Garden at Flushing
TYPE: UNCLEAR (rejected as a child of the bubble; the leading nursery was a casualty)
DATES: Robert Prince propagating by 1723; William in charge by 1745; catalogue 1771; Linnaean garden 1793; William Robert’s mulberry speculation and import of more than one million French trees in 1839; foreclosure by 1841. Parsons Nursery was founded in Flushing in 1838, during the boom, on the same soils and harbor that had already made the town a nursery district.
CHEAP INPUT: Mulberry trees at 2 to 4 cents each at the end of 1839 were unsaleable stock, not a new nursery’s raw material. Prince could not pay the mortgage.
HOW USED: The firm had sold fruit trees, ornamentals, and bulbs for a century. Winter, the creditor who took the garden, sold the plants and cut the ground into house lots. William Robert kept a smaller nursery next door and in 1846 published a rose manual.
MECHANISM: OTHER (a pre-existing trade hit by a product-line loss)
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Propagation skill survived in Flushing. The speculative tree inventory did not keep its value.
LAG: The crash and the foreclosure were one to two years apart (1839 to 1841).
FORESEEN?: A Massachusetts correspondent told an Ohio nurseryman in June 1836 that once supply caught up, trees would sell at the cost of growing them. That is the opposite of a belief that cheap trees would found a new industry.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: There was no railroad-style receivership, amalgamation, or nationalization, because there was no corporate network to take over. The people who had paid the high price mostly took the loss themselves, or walked away from contracts.

Merinos. Importers and the farmers who bought from them were both first and second claimants. After 1815 many sheep were butchered rather than resold. Later, Vermont owners sold commercial flocks west and kept, or rebuilt, the pedigreed remnant. Rams at $3,500 show that control of the best blood did not pass at a distress fraction of the 1810 price. A clean fraction of 1810 cost recovered in 1816 was not on a page fetched here. Cull sheep at 75 cents to $1 a head in 1867–70 are a different asset from an 1810 ram at $1,000.

Mulberries. Cheney Brothers went bankrupt on the trees and were carried by John and Seth Cheney; the family kept the mill. Prince lost the Linnaean garden by foreclosure by 1841 to Gabriel Winter, a relative by marriage, who sold the plants and subdivided the ground. The fraction of Prince’s mortgage recovered was not stated. End-of-1839 tree prices, 2 to 4 cents, were about 2 to 4 percent of the $1 to $1.25 price at the start of that year. Ohio trees offered at $5 per thousand in 1842 were the local floor.

Hens. Owners kept birds nobody would take, or ate them. No estate or court transferred a flock at a recorded fraction of the $120 pair.

Tulips. Goldgar found no bankrupt buyer. Buyers who had promised 100 or 1,000 guilders defaulted, which was a shock to credit and reputation. Thompson’s account is that officials converted futures into options so buyers could abandon the contract. The bulbs, as gardens, stayed with the growers. Production did not change hands.
SOURCE: https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/pubcho/v130y2007i1p99-114.html
QUOTE: "prices in futures contracts had been legally, albeit temporarily, converted into options exercise prices."
UNCERTAIN: A notarial sample of how many tulip contracts were settled in cash, in bulbs, or abandoned would pin down Thompson against Goldgar’s court records. Both were fetched only through a journal abstract and an interview, not through the underlying deeds.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: The bubbled asset was short-lived in every case. What reached the next generation was knowledge, a breed name, and cleared land, not a structure.

Sheep live long enough to shear for several years and then die or go to the knife. The 1810 cohort was not a standing plant in 1850. What survived was merino ancestry inside later flocks, and the Vermont pedigree as a brand. Fleece weight per head in Vermont rose from 2.2 pounds in 1840 to 5.34 pounds in 1870 while headcount fell. Cleared hill pasture outlived the flocks and was reused for cows. Mills, depots, and barns were built for the wool business; they were not the thing being speculated on. Many were later altered or dropped, which is why the National Register treats Vermont farm buildings as unstable evidence.

Multicaulis was sold as a cutting and was tender. The winter of 1840 killed large numbers in Connecticut, and the 1844 blight removed virtually all that remained. A few older white and black mulberries, from eighteenth-century planting, still stand. The Cheney mill building was manufacturing capital of 1838, financed at $50,000, not a warehouse of cheap trees.

Fancy fowl from 1849 were gone as a speculative stock by 1855. The Brahma and the standard book outlived them. Those are rules and a breed, not a flock carried forward at scrap value.

Tulip bulbs multiply or rot. The valuable “broken” patterns were a mosaic virus and could be kept only by offsets of the mother bulb, so they could not become a mass industrial input. Garber’s eighteenth-century prices show the flower trade still existed, with the hyacinth in the fashion lead by 1707.
SOURCE: https://connecticuthistory.org/connecticuts-mulberry-craze/
QUOTE: "In 1844 blight wiped out virtually all of the remaining multicaulis."

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The incumbents who lost were the people already selling the same product, plus the regions that could not match the new cost.

Spanish holders of the merino export ban lost the breeding monopoly once the Napoleonic Wars opened the flocks. In the United States the coarse “native” sheep lost the fine-wool job during the upgrade itself: by 1840 they were about 29 percent of New England sheep, merino grades about 70 percent, and English mutton breeds under 1 percent. After the late 1840s the losers were northeastern commercial wool growers. Vermont went from 1,681,819 sheep in 1840 to 297,000 in 1900. Western growers, at about 25 cents a head per year to keep, and Australian merinos, a separate branch of the Spanish exodus, took the wool market. After refrigerated meat, the Vermont merino itself lost to Southdown and other mutton types, in New England and in Australia, because the wrinkled carcass was the wrong shape for the London and Boston meat trade.

Household silk reelers in Mansfield, where three-quarters of households were in silk in 1825, lost to imports. Mills already preferred European and Asian raw silk in the 1820s because Connecticut reeling was uneven and gummy. The 1839 crash and the 1844 blight finished the cocoon side. Nurserymen who stayed long in multicaulis, Prince among them, lost the inventory and, in his case, the garden. Orren Bryant of Alexandria, Ohio, wound his tree sales up after 1842.

Buyers of Cochins and Shanghais lost the premium. Exhibition breeders later lost the claim to be the poultry industry, once integrated broilers, 90 percent of the supply by the mid-1960s, sold the meat.

Tulip buyers who refused to pay lost reputation. Goldgar did not find a class of ruined merchants or a displaced incumbent trade. Growers kept growing.
SOURCE: https://vermonthistory.org/william-jarvis-and-the-merino-sheep-craze
QUOTE: "The average annual cost of keeping a sheep in New England was $1.00-2.00 a head, while farmers further west were spending 25 cents a head."

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: The exclusion test holds. Each boom added supply inside the market it was built for: sheep for wool, fancy fowl for fanciers, mulberries for people who thought they would raise silkworms, bulbs for bulb buyers. That pattern favors the survivors of the same trade. It does not, by itself, found a downstream industry.

Cheney Brothers did move from trees to cloth, and the mill date, 1838, sits inside the craze. The pivot was away from the written-down input. Domestic cocoons were the wrong raw material before the crash, and the firm’s sewing silk was Asian. The cheapness of multicaulis after 1839 did not make Manchester a silk town. Imported fiber, thread machinery, and the wartime tariff on silk goods did. Mansfield’s mill, tied to local cocoons, failed with the speculation.

Merino flocks did become ordinary breeding stock after 1815, and that is why New England flocks were mostly grades by 1840. The customer was still a wool grower or a wool mill. The later Vermont trade sold rams at up to $3,500, which is a re-pricing of the scarce blood, not a giveaway. The cross-market residue is the pasture, used for dairy once wool was below the $1 to $2 keep and once rails and the 1854 ice car existed.

The tulip trade’s growers, notaries, and fanciers were already a horticultural network. Goldgar’s production result and Garber’s eighteenth-century bulb prices say that network continued at fundamental flower values. They do not say the crash seeded the modern Dutch export trade.

So the draft’s three “catalysis” sentences (mills funded by merino capital; hen fever causing broilers; multicaulis causing the nursery trade and the farm press) fail the test. The dairy switch and the Cheney pivot are the only large after-stories, and only the dairy used the written-down residue of the boom itself.
SOURCE: https://history.textiles.ncsu.edu/textile-companies/cheney-brothers-silk/
QUOTE: "it is the only mill established before that date that was permanently successful."
UNCERTAIN: “The only mill” is the 1916 company history’s claim, as reprinted by the textile-history page. A full census of pre-1838 silk sheds would test it. The Hanks shed and the Mansfield failure are on the same page.

PART C

1. The stock was consumed, not stranded. Rail and cable sit idle and can be rented. These organisms were eaten, burned, or infected. Fuller records merinos going to the butcher after 1815 once English wool was back. Connecticut trees were compost or pea brush, then blighted in 1844. Burnham’s end of the hen fever was a Shanghai dinner. A write-down that destroys the asset cannot cheapen a later user’s input.
SOURCE: https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1685&context=tsaconf
QUOTE: "The poor sheep found themselves served up as mutton as farmers off-loaded the excess stock"

2. Contemporaries who were in the trade already knew the metered input was the organism, not the fiber. In June 1836 an Ohio-bound nurseryman was told the tree business lasted only while prices stayed above the cost of growing. Pawley’s point is sharper: multicaulis was priced by the bud, the joint, and the inch, which is why it could be speculated in at all. Arthur Cole, quoted in the Connecticut account, grouped merinos, Berkshire hogs, Rohan potatoes, and mulberries as one family of exotic-stock crazes. The draft’s error is to treat the 1840s wool price as the merino crash.
SOURCE: https://resources.ohiohistory.org/ohj/search/display.php?ipp=20&page=111&pages=265-272&searchterm=Array&vol=45
QUOTE: "While the price of trees keeps up the business will be good, but probably in a few years there will be a supply and trees may be bought for what it costs to grow them."

3. A reproducible genetic asset does not stay cheap in its best lines. Ordinary merino grades flooded the wool market. Named Vermont rams were bid back up to $3,500, shipped west by the carload, and fought over in Australia as the “Vermont invasion,” then rejected once scouring and frozen mutton exposed the grease and the carcass. The crash socialized the breed and reconcentrated the rent in the pedigree. That is the opposite of a third-claim bargain.
SOURCE: https://vermonthistory.org/journal/85/VHS8501GreenMountainMerinos.pdf
QUOTE: "But our most profitable product is blood"

4. The machine shop, not the mulberry, is the non-obvious Cheney residue. Spencer’s carbine came out of a silk-mill tool room, about 200,000 guns, a loss, and a sale to Winchester. On the same mill floor, waste silk that moths had pierced was spun from 1855 after about $30,000 of machine changes. Both uses sat downstream of manufacturing skill and of imported fiber. Neither used a cheap tree. Easy to miss because the firm’s origin story is the craze.
SOURCE: https://connecticuthistory.org/the-cheney-brothers-rise-in-the-silk-industry/
QUOTE: "Developed in Cheney’s Manchester factory, this rifle boasted a revolutionary firing rate of 15 to 16 shots per minute."

5. The hen-fever standard pointed breeders at the wrong bird for meat. The APA’s own retrospective says the association was attacked for caring only about appearance. The birds that would not sell in 1855 were described as too tough to kill. The chickens that took the meat market were a Cornish–Plymouth Rock type of hybrid from the 1945–48 contests, raised by integrators who by the mid-1960s controlled about 90 percent of broilers. Linking Steele’s 1923 hatchery mistake to Burnham’s 1855 dinner is a story added later.
SOURCE: https://www.nationalchickencouncil.org/about-the-industry/history/
QUOTE: "By 1952, specially bred meat chickens (“broilers”) surpassed farm chickens as the number one source of chicken meat in the United States."
