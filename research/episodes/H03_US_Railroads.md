FACT: A3
VERDICT: PARTLY SUPPORTED
CORRECTION: Railroads did integrate inland counties into a national agricultural market. Donaldson and Hornbeck estimate that removing all railroads in 1890 would have cut the total value of US agricultural land by 60.2 percent, an annual loss of $386 million, or 3.22 percent of 1890 GNP, against Fogel’s upper-bound social saving of 2.7 percent of GNP ($321 million; his GNP is $12 billion), of which interregional agricultural freight was $73 million (0.6 percent) and intraregional freight $248 million (2.1 percent). The New York futures price tracked Chicago spot plus the lake-and-canal cost. All-rail wheat, Chicago to New York, fell from 38.61 cents a bushel in 1858 to 12.50 cents in 1896 (Newcomb), so a rail margin remained and shrank. It did not go to zero.

PART B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was a national railroad: road operated grew from 2,818 miles in 1840 to 70,268 miles in 1873 and 166,703 miles in 1890 (Df874), so about 96,400 of the 1890 miles, 58 percent, were added after the 1873 crash. In 1890 the roads carried 79,193 million freight ton-miles and 691 million revenue tons (Df901), an average haul of 114.6 miles (79,193 divided by 691). The metered input is average freight revenue per ton-mile, in cents. On a gold basis Newcomb gives 1.925 cents in 1867, 1.286 cents in 1877, 0.984 cent in 1887 and 0.806 cent in 1896, a fall of 58 percent. The all-railroad nominal series is 1.236 cents in 1882, 0.927 cent in 1890, 0.893 cent in 1893 and 0.746 cent in 1900 (NBER/FRED, matching Df908). A sample of thirteen large roads fell from 3.078 cents in 1865, a currency figure at the wartime peak, to 1.641 cents in 1873 and 0.803 cent in 1890. A specific lane, all-rail wheat from Chicago to New York, fell from 38.61 cents a bushel in 1858 to 12.50 cents in 1896. P*, the return the original securities had to earn, is not recovered from a prospectus in the pages read. The observable claim is the coupon and the dividend. On 57 reorganizations of 1884–99 the average coupon before reorganization was 5.78 percent (Schiffman, reporting Tufano). In the year ending 30 June 1897 Newcomb records interest on funded debt at 4.70 percent and dividends on stock at 1.62 percent; stock with par of $3,761,092,277, or 70.1 percent of stock, paid no dividend, and 43.7 percent of capitalization received neither dividend nor interest. Total par capitalization on that date was $10,635,008,074, under $60,000 a mile, on 184,428 miles. In 1890, property investment was $8,789 million against a capital account of $10,122 million, of which capital stock was $4,640 million and bonded debt $5,105 million (Df891–894). Physical idleness was not the main residue. Ton-miles on the thirteen-road sample rose from 7.48 billion in 1873 to 13.07 billion in 1879 while rates fell. Newcomb writes that probably no US railway, for any long period, carried as much as its full capacity, which is the ordinary slack of a high-fixed-cost plant. What did sit idle was unfinished line: Cotter quotes Sprague that construction was discontinued, “leaving a large mileage connecting nothing in particular.” Defaulted roads cut operating expenses about 14 percent, so the 1870s default wave reduced service. In 1894, ton-miles were 16.5 percent below 1893, and trackmen per 100 miles of line fell from 124 to 102. The tracks were run; the capital charge was what could not be earned.

BENEFICIARY: Commercial agriculture and inland shippers of grain and livestock
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY. Same market the mileage was built to serve. More capacity there predicts consolidation among the carriers, which is what happened, not a new industry. Donaldson and Hornbeck hold the freight rate fixed, so the land-value gain is from the network at Fogel’s rate, not from the later shortfall below P*.
DATES: Capitalization of market access measured on farm land, 1870 to 1890. Take-off of plains settlement follows each construction wave, especially after 1868.

All-rail wheat, Chicago to New York, fell from 38.61 cents a bushel in 1858 to 12.50 cents in 1896. Trunk corn in 1881–91 still moved more cheaply by water (7.7 cents a bushel) than by rail (14.6 cents).

MECHANISM: NEW-LOCATION

LAG: Continuous with construction. The 1870–90 land-value change is the measured window; it is not a lag after the 1893 crash.
FORESEEN?: Yes. Land-grant policy and western promoters sold the roads as the means of settling and shipping farm country. Newcomb in 1898 treats territorial specialization of wheat, cattle and wool as the established result.

BENEFICIARY: Dressed-beef meatpacking (G. F. Swift, Armour, Hammond, Morris)
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY. Shipping dressed beef rather than live cattle saves the freight on the inedible share, about 60 percent of the animal, and that saving is larger when the rate is high. The trade was already dominant before the 1893 write-down. Whether the small margin against local eastern slaughter required rates below the 1867 level is open.
DATES: Swift in Chicago 1875; Andrew Chase car and Grand Trunk trials, winter 1878; 12 New England branches by 1880; 43 branches and second-largest Chicago packer by 1884; incorporated 1885 at $300,000 capital; $35 million capital and $200 million sales in 1904. Chicago cattle shipped dressed rose from 38 percent in 1878 to 75 percent in 1887.
CHEAP INPUT: Freight on beef, Chicago to the East. Newcomb: the average rate on dressed beef, Chicago to New York, fell from 81 cents to 45 cents between 1872 and 1896 (the same passage prices packed meats per 100 pounds). Swift undercut local slaughter by 75 cents to $1 per 100 pounds, a 3 to 5 percent advantage. One car of dressed beef replaced about two and a half cars of live animals.
HOW USED: Kill in Chicago and later at Kansas City, Omaha, East St. Louis, St. Joseph, St. Paul and Fort Worth; move sides in the packer’s own iced cars; sell through branch houses to retail butchers. Meatpacking output rose from $62.1 million in 1870 (11th among manufactures; beef packing alone $1.95 million) to $790.3 million in 1900, second only to iron and steel.

SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The branch-house network, the private reefer fleet, brand (Swift Premium), and stockyard position. It kept a durable oligopoly: six firms dominated by 1903, Swift first, with $35 million of market capitalization.
LAG: About 5 years from the 1873 crash to the 1878 trials, and about 11 years from the 1867 Abilene railheads. The 1893 receiverships come about 15 years after take-off.
FORESEEN?: No, by the people in the trade. Swift’s partner Hathaway called the scheme unsound and they split. Eastern trunk lines, invested in stock cars and pens, refused the traffic. The Grand Trunk took it because it had little livestock business. Local butchers then fought the product. By 1898 Newcomb treats “beef raised in Texas and killed in Chicago” as ordinary.

TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY. Oranges are high value per pound and the binding constraint in the 1906 case is speed and a guaranteed through rate, plus seasonal cars, not a rate below the builders’ coupon. Rebates show large shippers also skimmed the competitive margin.

BENEFICIARY: Cattle drives to railheads (Abilene and the cowtowns)
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY, and only in the designed market. This is livestock freight, the traffic stock cars and the Evener pool were built to carry. It is not a third claim. QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: Control moved by equity receivership, foreclosure and reorganization, not by nationalization and not by a general order of open access. There was no federal bankruptcy statute for these firms until 1898. In more than 90 percent of receiverships from 1867 to 1897 the sitting managers were appointed receivers. Bondholders, through private bankers led by J. P. Morgan and Kuhn Loeb, wrote the plans. On 57 receiverships from 1884 to 1899, fixed charges were cut 35 percent, bond principal 22 percent, and the average coupon from 5.78 percent to 4.72 percent. Income bonds replaced some fixed coupons. Voting trusts were used in over 50 percent of 1890s railroad reorganizations, typically for five years or until dividends resumed, with the bankers keeping a board seat. Stockholders, not bondholders, paid most of the cash assessments: of $96 million assessed in 37 of the 57 cases, stockholders paid $87 million. By 1906 seven communities of interest controlled two-thirds of national mileage and 85 percent of operating revenue. Newcomb’s foreclosure flow for 1894–97 is 38,879 miles with par capital of $2,748,847,000. That is a par figure, not a cash price. Federal operation comes later, December 1917 to March 1920, and is outside this bust. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 banned rebates and pooling; it did not hand the network to shippers.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: The long-lived part is the alignment: grade, right-of-way, bridges and city terminals. Newcomb’s point is that a line opened with light iron rail, steep grades and sharp curves, and that the later steel roadway, heavier rail, yards and stations were added during its working life. Commercial US steel-rail production began in 1867, when rails cost $120.12 a ton. In 1880 the price was $67.50 and 29 percent of track was steel; in 1890 the price was $31.75 and 80 percent was steel; in 1896 the price was $28 and 12 percent of track was still iron. So the rail itself, and the rolling stock, were replaced, not inherited from the 1840s or from 1873. Locomotives numbered 15.6 thousand in 1876 (Df877) and 35,986 on 30 June 1897, with 1,221,730 freight cars owned by the railroads, plus private cars not in that count. In fiscal 1897, maintenance of way and structures was 21.22 percent of operating expenses and maintenance of equipment 16.34 percent: both were being consumed and renewed while the beneficiaries grew. Of the 166,703 miles operated in 1890, 70,268 had existed in 1873. The plant Swift, the millers and the citrus shippers used was largely the 1880s network, re-railed in steel, and then partly relieved of its coupons after 1893.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The clearest displaced incumbents are local wholesale butchers. Levi Samuels told the Senate committee in 1890 that in nearly every small town, dressed beef had stopped local slaughter, and that a butcher who would not buy western dressed beef had nothing else to buy. Live-cattle shippers and the trunk lines’ Evener pool (Erie, Pennsylvania, New York Central, 1875–79) lost the eastbound livestock trade as dressed shipments from Chicago went from 38 percent of the cattle in 1878 to 75 percent in 1887. Counties that already had good water access in 1870 saw a relative decline in farm land values as rail equalized market access (Donaldson and Hornbeck). Railroad stockholders and junior bondholders lost the capital charge: in the year to 30 June 1897, 70.1 percent of stock paid no dividend. Water carriers on the trunk grain route did not lose it. Coleman’s open-season share for Chicago corn by lake is about 95 percent, at 7.7 cents a bushel against 14.6 cents by rail, and Newcomb says total water movement rose while rail rose. Canal boatmen were not, on these pages, the main losers to cheap rail freight.

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: Gold-basis average freight revenue per ton-mile, Newcomb, from the USDA freight-rate section: 1.925 cents in 1867, 1.286 cents in 1877, 0.984 cent in 1887, 0.806 cent in 1896. The 1867 currency figure was 2.678 cents, which he converts to gold before comparing. Nominal all-railroad revenue per ton-mile (NBER/FRED and Df908): 1.236 cents in 1882, 0.927 cent in 1890, 0.893 cent in 1893, 0.852 cent in 1894, 0.746 cent in 1900. The thirteen-road sample, lower because it is large roads: 3.078 cents in 1865, 1.802 cents in 1870, 1.641 cents in 1873, 0.902 cent in 1879, 0.803 cent in 1890. Average haul rose only from about 101 miles in 1867 to 124.47 miles in 1896, and from 109.2 miles in 1882 (39,302 million ton-miles divided by 360 million tons) to 114.6 miles in 1890 (79,193 divided by 691). Haul length is too small a change to explain the per-ton-mile fall. Tons carried one mile per freight-train mile rose from 80.77 in 1867 to 134.83 in 1880, 175.12 in 1890 and 198.81 in 1896, about two and a half times, while the gold rate fell 58 percent. Steel rail and heavier trains are Newcomb’s technical channel. The operating ratio fell to 58.36 percent in 1880 and then rose to 65.80 percent in 1890 and 67.20 percent in 1896, so after 1880 the rate cut outran the recorded cost cut and the margin left for capital shrank. Newcomb’s own split is that density and those physical economies made the lower rate possible, and that competition among cities, markets and producers forced the saving through to shippers. He argues that wars among parallel routes wasted money (he cites twenty-one Chicago–New York routes) and tended to prop up the average by pushing local rates, rather than to lower it. The trunk-line pools, the Evener rebates, and the citrus bonuses of $10 to $40 a car show that competitive through rates did fall below published tariffs. The 35 percent fixed-charge cut of 1884–99 made the already-reduced rate livable for the reorganized mileage; it comes after most of the 1867–87 decline (1.925 cents to 0.984 cent).

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: Receivership under a federal judge, then a banker-run reorganization, then usually the same properties in the hands of the old senior creditors with a lighter coupon. Managers stayed as receivers in over 90 percent of cases from 1867 to 1897. Morgan and Kuhn Loeb became the reorganization managers. For 57 firms, 1884–99, fixed charges fell 35 percent, principal 22 percent, and the average interest rate from 5.78 percent to 4.72 percent. About 10.6 percentage points of the fixed-charge cut came from swapping fixed coupons for income bonds. Stockholders were assessed for the new cash ($87 million of $96 million). Voting trusts, in over half of the 1890s reorganizations, voted the stock for about five years. The network’s required earnings fell by that 35 percent on the reorganized mileage, which at the 1894 peak was on the order of a quarter of the system. The owners of the claims changed less than the claims did: by 1906, seven communities of interest held two-thirds of the mileage. Equity was assessed and diluted. It was not, on these figures, uniformly extinguished, and Schiffman notes Daggett’s finding of large short-run returns to stockholders who paid the assessment in eight 1893–98 cases.

PART C

1. Trunk grain did not switch to the cheap railroad. In 1881–91 lake-and-canal corn, Chicago to New York, averaged 7.7 cents a bushel against 14.6 cents by rail, and about 95 percent of open-season shipments from Chicago went by lake. The futures price was built on the water rate. Rail was the feeder and the winter route. A write-down of rail capital was not the cheap input on the main export haul.

2. Dressed beef is the wrong way around for a simple “rate collapse created the industry” story. Not shipping the inedible 60 percent saves more dollars when the rate is higher, and one dressed car replaced about two and a half live cars. The roads that owned the stock pens refused the traffic. The Grand Trunk, with little livestock investment and a route the trunk-line pool could not police, took it in 1878. Chicago dressed shipments were already 75 percent of the cattle by 1887. The 1893 receiverships are late for this industry.

3. The official freight-rate statistician in 1898 attributes the 58 percent gold decline, 1.925 cents to 0.806 cent, to density and to the train as a machine: ton-miles per train-mile rose from 80.77 in 1867 to 198.81 in 1896, and steel rail went from a commercial novelty in 1867 to 80 percent of track in 1890. He argues that wars among parallel routes were wasteful and worked against a lower average. 4. The outcome the same-market residue predicts is consolidation, and that is what the 1890s produced. Voting trusts in over half of reorganizations, then seven communities of interest with two-thirds of the mileage and 85 percent of operating revenue by 1906. The purchasers were the old senior creditors and their bankers, at a 22 percent principal cut and a 35 percent cut in fixed charges, not a new class of downstream operators.

5. Two non-obvious captures of the surplus, and one limit on the 1870s version of “the tracks remained and ran cheap.” Private car lines took $10 to $40 a car from connecting roads for routing fruit, and passed a quarter to a half on to the shipper; the scarce asset was the car, and the 1900 routing rule was written to stop the leak. Ice and packer byproducts, the latter growing to roughly the size of the beef trade, were industries inside the cold chain, easy to miss if one looks only at catalogs and brands. Separately, Cotter finds that 1870s receivers, lacking a settled power to issue receivers’ certificates, cut operating expenses about 14 percent when they defaulted. The idle claim was not only financial in that decade. The later equity receivership is what kept a written-down network running. Land-grant roads, built into thin country, were 18 to 23 percent more likely to default than other roads in his linear-probability models, which is the postwar “ahead of demand” margin, not an 1840s story.
