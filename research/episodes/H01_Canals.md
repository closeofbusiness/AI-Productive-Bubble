QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: Britain’s canal companies had about £17 million invested between 1755 and 1815, more than the roughly £10 million in turnpike roads by the early 1820s. No mileage total was on a page fetched this session. The metered input is the freight rate per ton-mile, toll plus carriage. Around 1790 it was about 0.38 shillings per ton-mile in current prices (about 0.25 shillings in 1700 prices). Around 1840 it was about 0.20 shillings per ton-mile (about 0.12 shillings in 1700 prices). In 1810, Zachariah Allnutt’s heavy-goods rates on one group of canals were about 3.25 to 4.25 pence per ton-mile, with valuable goods marked up 41 to 54 percent. On five routes into Manchester in 1777, canal rates were 20 to 40 shillings per ton against road rates of 60 to 93 shillings per ton.

P* was met or beaten on the canals that carried the industrial traffic. The Bridgewater Act capped the Liverpool–Manchester toll at 2s. 6d. per ton; by the 1820s the trustees’ effective charge, Sandars wrote, was 5s. 2d. per ton, and Sandars put the Duke’s net income at nearly £100,000 a year for twenty years. A Quarterly Review article of March 1825, quoted by Pratt, gives the Birmingham Canal a dividend of £140 a year on an original £140 share, and shares up to £3,200. In December 1824 a Trent and Mersey share of £75 was quoted at £2,200. Bogart reports an 8 percent average equity return for 1770–1850 on five better canals in Arnold and McCartney’s sample, and an average dividend near 6 percent of capital for eighty canals in the 1820s. The mania price was a different and failed P*: Grand Junction at £472.75 in October 1792 against £100 par, back near par by 1795, a write-down of the premium by the same shareholders, not a collapse of the toll below construction cost.

The speculative tranche missed any normal return. The same 1825 article says a large proportion scarcely paid interest of 1 percent, and many paid nothing. That is empty or thin traffic, not a measured share of ton-miles standing idle. The busy coal canals were the opposite of idle. After railways, tolls on some lines were cut far below the old dividend-supporting rates: the Kennet and Avon, which had paid 5 percent, was taken down to 1¼d., then 1d., then ½d. per ton-mile, and the cuts brought no extra traffic.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: In Britain the 1793 loss was mostly a write-down of share premiums by the same owners. Grand Junction fell from £472.75 in October 1792 to around £100 by 1795. The companies kept the canals and went on building. There was no general receivership. Parliament capped tolls and left carriage, after the Bridgewater generation, to independent boatmen who paid those tolls. From 1845 Parliament allowed railway amalgamation, lease, purchase or guarantee. Pratt says about one-third of canal mileage had passed under railway control by the time that power was curtailed in 1873 and 1888. The Kennet and Avon, stated cost £1,000,000 in 1794, was taken by the Great Western around 1851–52 for an annuity of £7,773 a year; the railway’s capital account for it stood at £209,510 by December 1905, about 21 percent of the stated £1,000,000. The Shropshire Union was leased in perpetuity to the London and North Western in 1847, at a rent equal to half that railway’s dividend rate, on share capital of £482,924 and debt of £814,207. The Birmingham Canal was not sold cheap: the London and Birmingham guaranteed £4 a share. British nationalisation was not on any page fetched.

In America the Erie stayed with New York. Of about $7 million of canal debt issued in 1817–25, only $1.25 million was still outstanding in 1841. Tolls were abolished in 1882 after the Erie had paid for itself, maintained itself, subsidised lateral canals, and contributed to the state. Pennsylvania kept its Main Line, defaulted in August 1842 on a debt of over $36 million, resumed interest in February 1845, and on 25 June 1857 sold the Main Line at auction to the Pennsylvania Railroad for $7.5 million, about 21 percent of that 1842 debt stock, which includes borrowing to pay interest and is not a pure construction cost. Indiana defaulted in January 1841 and resumed in July 1847. Its 1836 plan had expected canal tolls of only $120,000 in 1842 against interest of $420,000, so the creditors’ shortfall was a land-tax failure, not a fire sale of a completed canal.
UNCERTAIN: what share of Pennsylvania’s $7.5 million equals original construction outlay alone. Worthington’s finance history, which Wallis cites, would settle it. The Illinois and Michigan Canal’s later lease or sale was not on a page fetched.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Almost all of the capital was long-lived substrate: cuttings, embankments, tunnels, aqueducts, lock masonry and reservoirs. Boats, horses, lock gates and pumps were the short-lived part. On the Kennet and Avon, lock walls 3 to 6 feet thick were still in use when Pratt inspected them about 1905, and a boat still needed about three hours to pass the 29 locks at Devizes. The industrial users of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries used that masonry while the original companies still owned the profitable lines. Failed mania canals also left earthworks, but without traffic they were not an input. Railway ownership later kept some of the substrate open at a loss. In the half-year to 31 December 1905 the Great Western’s canals took in £6,582 against expenses of £12,794; the Kennet and Avon alone took in £2,035 against expenses of £5,604.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The losers were not the people who had previously hauled coal long distances by road. Bogart, Lefors and Satchell find that opening ordinary barge service did not reduce the number of road-carrier services. Heavy goods over any distance were a new trade. The losers of the express canal were the road carriers of merchandise: a fly boat cut their weekly London services by about 43 to 48 percent. At the start, pack-horse owners and turnpike trustees opposed canals, and the Mersey and Irwell Navigation fought the Bridgewater to protect its tolls. Canal companies themselves later lost high-value and much bulk traffic to railways, which traders welcomed as an escape from canal tolls. Coastal coal from the Tyne to London was not displaced. In America, New Orleans lost the western grain flow: by 1846 Buffalo shipped more flour and wheat, and a federal report for 1860 said probably only one-fifth of Ohio Valley produce still went to New Orleans. Philadelphia lost the position of commercial emporium to New York. Montreal’s claim on the lakes was the route the Erie was built to head off.

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: The Erie Canal, authorised in 1817 and opened in 1825, was state-financed and did not default. New York dedicated auction duties and a salt tax before the canal earned; in 1824 those two taxes yielded $290,000 against canal-bond interest of $350,000, and tolls were already above plan. Downstream, New York City, Buffalo elevators, Rochester milling, lake boats and lakeshore farming all grew on a canal that covered its cost. Ohio, which also did not default, had canal earnings of $150,000 in 1833, enough to pay interest and return $47,000 to the state.

Eight states and the territory of Florida defaulted in 1841 and 1842. The transport defaulters include Pennsylvania (August 1842), Maryland (January 1842), Indiana (January 1841), Illinois, and Michigan (July 1841). Pennsylvania’s Main Line, opened in 1834, never covered its interest: in 1835 tolls were $684,357 against interest of $1,169,455, on a debt of almost $25 million. By the 1842 default the debt was over $36 million. Shippers on that line received transport cheaper than the debt required, which is the third-claim condition, and Philadelphia still lost the western trade to New York. The state sold the works to the Pennsylvania Railroad for $7.5 million on 25 June 1857. By 1901 nearly all of Pennsylvania’s state canals were shut. Indiana had authorised up to $10 million in 1836 for a population of about 600,000 and ordinary revenue of about $50,000. Construction stopped in 1839 when the banks that had bought its bonds on credit failed. The state had planned on tolls of $120,000 in 1842 against interest of $420,000, and on property tax. It defaulted because land values fell, from $9.87 per acre in 1837 to $3.73 in 1842, not because a finished cheap canal created a new economy. Ohio shows the contrast: the state that kept its canal debt afloat had the canal that paid.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: Railway companies did acquire canals, often because the canal company blocked a railway bill or threatened to build a parallel railway. They did not, as a rule, buy them cheaply in order to run them as a new low-price utility. Pratt, reporting James Inglis of the Great Western in 1905, says that company owned about 216 miles, not a mile of it acquired voluntarily. The Kennet and Avon, construction stated at £1,000,000 in 1794, once paid 5 percent. Around 1851 the Great Western took it and paid £7,773 a year, and thereafter lost about £4,000 a year on it. The Board of Trade then cut the canal rate to 1¼d., then 1d., then ½d. per ton-mile, and traffic did not return. By the early twentieth century the western end carried about three or four boats a day. The Somerset Coal Canal, independent, was abandoned by Parliament; the Wilts and Berks, also independent, was practically derelict. The Shropshire Union lease of 1847 kept a canal system going under the London and North Western rather than closing it. The Birmingham arrangement was a 4 percent guarantee, not a shutdown purchase. So railways both inherited canals and, on lines such as the Kennet and Avon, presided over their commercial death. Cutting the toll below the old P* did not produce a new downstream industry.

PART C

1. The coal-price fall and the canal owner’s profit moved together. Manchester coal was halved by 1762 and the Duke’s profits rose. The industrial input became cheaper than the road, and dearer than a competitive toll. That is the opposite of a third claim, in which the builder’s required price is the one that fails.
SOURCE: https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2014/06/crisis-chronicles-canal-mania-1793/
QUOTE: "even as the price of coal halved, the Duke’s profits soared."

2. Ordinary canal barges did not destroy road carriers. They created a heavy-goods trade that had not existed. Fly boats, a later organisation of relays and night running, did the destroying, and they charged more than slow boats, not less. Accounts that treat “the canal” as one cheaper substitute for the wagon miss both the new market and the express market.
SOURCE: https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dbogart/canals_creative_destruction_forthcoming_aug222018.pdf
QUOTE: "barges did not displace wagon services" is the finding; the paper’s sentence is: "canal barge services provided a new transportation service for heavy goods."

3. The mania’s written-down residue is the wrong network. Canals tied to real trade paid. Of the 44 new-canal Acts of 1791–95, only a few made money. A large share of canals barely paid 1 percent or paid nothing. The cotton, metal, pottery and salt trades sat on the first group. More empty cuts predict idle earthworks, not a new industry.
SOURCE: https://canalrivertrust.org.uk/things-to-do/canal-history/history-features-and-articles/the-first-canal-age-canal-history
QUOTE: "only a few were to make money for their investors."

4. When tolls were later forced below the old profitable level, new users did not appear. On the Kennet and Avon the rate fell to ½d. per ton-mile and Inglis said there was never a sign of extra traffic. The cheap post-railway canal is a failed third-claim experiment.
SOURCE: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/47435/47435-h/47435-h.htm
QUOTE: "finally ½d. per ton-mile; but there had never been a sign, Mr Inglis added, that the reduction had any effect in attracting additional traffic."

5. In the United States the downstream geography follows the canal that covered P*, not the canals that defaulted. New York did not default; Pennsylvania’s tolls in 1835 were $684,357 against interest of $1,169,455, and the line was sold to the Pennsylvania Railroad in 1857 for $7.5 million and then largely shut. Indiana did not even plan to service its debt from tolls. A write-down of unfinished or unremunerative canals did not reproduce Buffalo, Rochester or New York.
