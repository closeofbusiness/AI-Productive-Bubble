## H02 — UK Railway Mania (1844–47) → `H02_UK_Railway_Mania.md`

- **Built:** ~9,500 miles authorised 1844–47, ~half built by early 1850s; open mileage 2,240 (1844) → 6,621 (1850). Peak investment £44m in 1847 ≈ 8% of GDP ✅ (Mitchell: 6.7%).
- **Metered input:** freight ~0.15s/ton-mile (c.1845) → ~0.10s (c.1865); third-class fare ≈1d/mile (statutory ceiling from **1844**, i.e. boom-era, not bust-era).
- **P\*:** 1845 plans assumed £15,600/mile cost, £1,670/mile revenue, 7% dividends ✅. Actual 1850: £35,200/mile capital, £2,080/mile revenue; ordinary dividends ~2–3% 1849–58.
- **Route to cheapness:** *write-down without transfer* — market prices fell ~60% (index 167.9 → 60.5), few bankruptcies (Parliament limited leverage), shareholders kept the lines, amalgamation into the large companies.
- **Third claims:** (1) freight shippers — the prospectuses priced a *passenger* railway; passengers 68% of revenue (1844) → 47% (1853); contemporaries "surprised" by freight; (2) **London's long-distance milk trade** — 96% of London's liquid milk by rail by 1914 ✅, carried at rates the companies called unremunerative.
- **Diffusion:** W H Smith bookstalls (1848), Thomas Cook (1841, pre-mania), Bass/Burton (1839 line, pre-crash), suburbs (location rent near stations), football specials (1900s), Royal Mail (1838 Act).
- **Rejected as downstream:** Deloitte/audit, Railway Clearing House, railway time — services/institutions *of* the railways (builder-side), not users of the cheap input.
- **Scarce complement:** milk — wholesale contracts, cooling, the retail round; Bass — water, brand, tied trade; suburbs — land near stations.
- **Substrate vs overlay:** iron (not steel) rails lasted ~3.5 years on busy lines; earthworks, tunnels, viaducts, right-of-way are the century-scale asset.
- **Lag:** 0–6 yrs (freight mix); ~20–25 yrs (milk take-off); 40–55 yrs (football specials).
- **Losers:** stagecoaches and turnpikes, canal high-value traffic, coastal passenger steam, urban cowkeepers (~20,000 cows within 8–9 miles of the Royal Exchange, early 1850s).

## H03 — US railroad booms and receiverships (1850s–1890s) → `H03_US_Railroads.md`

- **Built:** 9,021 miles operated (1850) → 70,268 (1873) → 166,703 (1890); 58% of the 1890 network added after the 1873 crash. No US "mania" in the 1840s (synthesis wrong).
- **Metered input:** freight revenue per ton-mile (gold) 1.925¢ (1867) → 0.806¢ (1896), –58%; explained mostly by density and heavier trains (ton-miles per train-mile 80.8 → 198.8) and steel rail, not by the write-down.
- **P\*:** observable as the coupon: 5.78% average pre-reorganisation on 57 firms; 1897: 70% of stock paid no dividend.
- **Route:** *write-down without (much) transfer* — equity receiverships, managers as receivers in >90% of cases, Morgan/Kuhn Loeb reorganisations: fixed charges –35% ✅, principal –22%, coupon 5.78% → 4.72%; ~23% of mileage under receivers in 1894; then **consolidation** — seven "communities of interest" with ⅔ of mileage and 85% of revenue by 1906 ✅.
- **Third claims (strict):** only the **private refrigerator/fruit car lines** — collected $10–40/car routing bonuses from duplicate competing roads (a leak of the overbuilt network's competition).
- **Diffusion:** commercial agriculture (Donaldson–Hornbeck: removing rail in 1890 cuts farmland value 60.2% ≈ 3.22% of GNP), **dressed-beef meatpacking** (Swift, Armour; 38% → 75% of Chicago cattle shipped dressed 1878–87; Swift undercut local slaughter 75¢–$1/100 lb ✅), Minneapolis milling, New England woollens on western wool, California citrus, packer by-products, manufactured ice, McCormick-type national distribution.
- **Not classified (source timeouts):** Montgomery Ward, Sears, RFD, Parcel Post, packaged-goods brands — **open gap**.
- **Scarce complement:** branch-house networks, private reefer fleets, brands (Swift Premium), farmland in newly accessible counties.
- **Non-obvious:** dressed beef came via the Grand Trunk — the road *without* livestock investment; the trunk lines that owned stock pens refused the traffic; Swift's own partner called it unsound.
- **Losers:** local wholesale butchers, live-cattle shippers and the Evener pool, water-access counties (relative land values).

## H04 — Steamships and refrigerated shipping (1870–1913) → `H04_Steamships_Refrigeration.md`

- **Built:** UK steam tonnage 3.0m (1881) → 11.0m tons (1912); Suez (1869); coaling stations. Hulls iron then **steel from ~1890** ✅ (synthesis's "iron-hulled" misdates the later fleet).
- **Metered input:** tramp freight index (1869 = 100) to 45 (Isserlis, 1908) / 35.4 (Klovland, 1904); real freights ~half of 1869 by 1906–10.
- **Route:** *technological deflation of the new vintage* — cheaper hulls and shipbuilding = 25–35% of the freight fall ✅, productivity 35–50%; i.e. a **lower P\* for each new ship**, not a write-down of old ones. Below-cost windows only in cycle troughs (1884–86, 1901–08), captured by whoever chartered then.
- **Third claims:** none clean. Every named user is diffusion.
- **Diffusion:** Atlantic grain (Liverpool–Chicago gap 60.2% → 14.2%, 1870–1912 ✅), Danish co-op butter and bacon on cheap feed grain, Argentine and NZ frozen/chilled meat (Vestey, Swift, Armour later), United Fruit bananas, British coal exports (backhaul joint-cost pricing), mass migration, jute, Japanese exports via Suez.
- **Scarce complement:** *refrigerated tonnage and the vertical cold chain* (rationed by freight committee; US firms held 58.5% of reserved reefer space in 1914), co-operative organisation (Denmark), concessions + fleet (United Fruit).
- **Counter-evidence:** instrumented freight explains little of the 1870–1913 trade boom (Jacks–Pendakur; income growth ~76%); first NZ frozen meat moved on a *sailing* ship; sail still competitive on some routes in 1914.

## H07 — Telegraph booms (US 1846–66; Atlantic cable; UK nationalisation 1870) → `H07_Telegraph.md`

- **Built:** 75 US companies / 21,147 miles of wire by 1851; Western Union 97,594 wire-miles (1868) → 874,420 (1898); Atlantic cables 1858 (failed), 1866 (lasting).
- **Metered input:** US average toll per message $1.09 (1867) → 30¢ (1900); NY–Chicago 10 words $1.55 (1850) → $0.40 (1890); Atlantic £1/word (1866) → 1s/word (1888); UK inland 1s/20 words → 6d/12 words (1885).
- **Route:** mostly *profitable consolidation* (Western Union kept 30–40¢ of each revenue dollar as profit ✅) — not a write-down; the clean write-off is only the 1858 cable (~£500k). UK: nationalised at a **premium** (20 years' purchase), then priced by statute below cost.
- **Third claim:** **British provincial newspapers on the statutory press rate** — 1s per 100 words at night ✅ (one-fifth of the ordinary rate per word), loss to the Post Office £375k in 1900; papers taking telegraph news 144 → 365. *A political price, not bubble residue.*
- **Diffusion:** cotton merchants (Steinwender: price-gap variance –90%, efficiency gain ≈8% of export value — from *speed* at the opening £1/word tariff, not the later price fall), CBOT grain futures, NYSE and brokers, Swift/Armour (~$200k/yr each in telegrams), news agencies (AP 1848, Reuters, Press Association 1865), tramp shipping (less time in port), FitzRoy storm warnings (1861).
- **Unclear/builder-side:** railways' train dispatch (avoided double-tracking ≈$957m in 1893 vs WU book capital $123m ✅), ticker (Gold & Stock 1867, bought by WU 1871).
- **Non-obvious:** a whole **code-book industry** existed *because* the ocean word stayed dear — a business on the scarcity, which the later price fall killed.

## H10 — Telecom/fibre boom and bust (1996–2002) → `H10_Fibre_Telecom.md`

- **Built:** US national route-miles 104,444 (1996) → 396,125 (2001), only ~52% owned (rest IRU swaps); ~$90bn in four years; capacity ~500× vs demand ~4× (1997–2001, Odlyzko).
- **Metered input:** US IP transit $1,200/Mbps-month (1998) → $200 (2002) → $25 (2007) → $0.63 (2015) (DrPeering, "very rough"); NY–LA OC-3 $1.8m/yr → <$150k/yr (Q1 2002).
- **P\*:** Qwest sold ~half its fibres for ~$3.6bn ≈ 90% of construction cost; dark fibre ~$5,000/mile (1997) → $1,200 (2001).
- **Route:** *write-down with transfer* — the clean case: Global Crossing to ST Telemedia for $250m (≈1.8% of reported $13.7bn build), FLAG to Reliance $207m, WilTel to Level 3, PSINet US to Cogent for ~$10m → 100 Mbps at $1,000/month ✅. Equity cancelled; IRUs handed back.
- **Third claims:** YouTube (2005), Netflix instant viewing (Jan 2007), Google's private dark fibre (replication, video), **US research & education networks** (FiberCo/NLR: >27,000 route-miles by 2005, incl. 8,000-mile AT&T donation to SURA), **Indian ITES/call centres** (employment CAGR 52.6% FY2000–05; distinct from software exports), wholesale VoIP (Global Crossing VoIP minutes ×15, 2001–03), Cogent's customers.
- **Diffusion:** Indian software exports (labour story, predates glut), Skype/consumer VoIP, Akamai (a *scarcity-era* business), Cloudflare (**contested** — see conflict note), podcasts, music streaming, SaaS/ASPs (bandwidth <1% of cost).
- **Threshold rule (Grok, B8):** the glut was decisive only where consumption is **continuous and bulk** and the product is priced at **a few dollars per user per month** (2 h/day at 1 Mbps ≈ $100/month at 1998 transit vs ≈$2 at 2007).
- **Scarce complement:** audience and upload habit (YouTube), ratings + studio licences + subscriber list (Netflix), index + ad auction (Google), English-speaking graduates (ITES), campus demand + skill to light glass (universities).
- **Substrate vs overlay:** ≥⅔ of the cost of usable bandwidth was the lighting electronics ✅; the glass and conduit transferred, the electronics were replaced.
- **Correction carried to the framework:** the "2.7%" was Merrill Lynch's macro-US **lit-capacity utilisation** (Jul 2001) vs a ~15% 1980s norm ✅ — not "97% dark", not "single-city".

## H01 — Canal Mania (UK 1790s) and the US canal boom / 1841–42 defaults → `H01_Canals.md`

- **Built:** ~£17m invested in UK canals 1755–1815; 44 new-canal Acts 1791–95, "only a few" made money; Grand Junction shares £100 → £472.75 (Oct 1792) → ~£100 (1795). US: Erie (1817–25, ~$7m debt); Pennsylvania Main Line (debt >$36m by 1842).
- **Metered input:** canal freight ~0.38s/ton-mile (c.1790) ≈ ⅓ of road (~1.46s); ~0.20s (c.1840). Erie: Buffalo–New York ~$12/ton vs ~$100 overland.
- **Route:** the cheap input came from the **canals that paid** (Bridgewater halved Manchester coal by 1762 *while* the Duke's profits soared; Birmingham Canal paid £140/yr on a £140 share) — i.e. *a new technology priced at a profitable P\**, not a write-down. The mania's write-down was of share **premiums**, by the same owners.
- **Third claims:** effectively **none**. The written-down residue was the wrong network: the Kennet & Avon toll cut to ½d/ton-mile attracted no new traffic ✅; Pennsylvania's Main Line carried shippers below P\* (1835 tolls $684k vs interest $1.17m ✅) and founded no new trade; sold to the PRR for $7.5m (1857), canals shut by 1901.
- **Diffusion:** Lancashire coal users and Manchester cotton, Birmingham metal trades, Staffordshire potteries (Wedgwood was a *promoter* — first claim), Burton brewers, Cheshire salt, Pickfords' fly boats (non-obvious *service* business — express merchandise, relays, night running), lime/clay/stone trades; US: New York City as port and money market, Great Lakes farming, Buffalo grain elevators (Dart 1842), Rochester milling, lake shipping.
- **Scarce complement:** location (Buffalo junction, Genesee water power, New York harbour), land, brands (Wedgwood, Burton water).
- **Lesson:** **C2 in action** — write-down capacity without surviving output demand founds nothing; downstream geography follows the asset that covered P\*. Railways later bought ~⅓ of UK canal mileage, "not a mile … acquired voluntarily" ✅ — to neutralise, not to exploit.

## H12 — Early petroleum (Pennsylvania 1859–1870s) → `H12_Petroleum.md`

- **Built:** wells, wooden tankage, stills, pipe; US refining capacity 46,571 bbl/day (1872) ≈ 3× production ("a refining capacity of three barrels to every one produced").
- **Metered input:** crude $20/bbl (1859) → $0.10 (end-1861) → ~$1 range 1874–1900; NY kerosene 44.75¢/gal (1863) → 5.24¢ (1893).
- **Route:** *write-down by distressed sale* — Standard bought 21–22 of 26 Cleveland refineries in 1872 at ~40–45% of sellers' own valuation ✅ (second claim), then consolidation.
- **Third claims — the by-product mechanism:** kerosene was the target product; the **joint products** were priced at disposal value — naphtha "burned under stills … thrown away or sold at the purchaser's price" ✅, gasolene, still residuum. Users: **paint/varnish solvent makers**, **automatic gas machines** for buildings off the gas mains ✅, **mill and railway lubricants / mineral sperm** (displacing whale and lard oil), **paraffin-wax and candle works** buying refinery bottoms, **Chesebrough's Vaseline** (patent 1872, from still residuum), rhigolene for surgical cooling.
- **Same-market residue → consolidation:** kerosene lighting itself (lamp buyers were the builders' customers) → Standard Oil.
- **Rejected ("just in time for the ICE"):** WRONG ✅ — Pennsylvania peaked 1891 and fell 14m barrels by 1894; motoring rode Spindletop (1901), Midcontinent and California. Autos, tractors, trucking, aviation, petroleum asphalt (post-1902) belong to the *later* boom; 1870s asphalt avenues used Trinidad bitumen.
- **Scarce complement:** for by-product users — formulas, patents and brand (Vaseline), process know-how (Merrill's deodorising); for kerosene — Standard's distribution and export brands.
- **Losers:** whale/sperm oil (already declining), camphene and alcohol lamp fuels (helped by an 1862–65 alcohol tax), coal-oil refiners, teamsters (~$4/bbl haul replaced by ~$1 pipe), independent Cleveland refiners.

## H05 — Bicycle mania (UK and US, 1895–98) → `H05_Bicycles.md`

- **Built:** manufacturing capacity, not a network: £17.3m of cycle promotions in 1896 + £7.4m in 1897 (~⅛ of all UK issues in 1896); UK output ~750,000 bicycles at the 1896 peak; US ~1–2m in 1897 (~1% of GNP).
- **Metered input:** £/bicycle and $/bicycle — Rudge-Whitworth cut prices 44%/37% in July 1897; Van Cleve $60–65 (1896) → $32–47 (1900).
- **Crash:** cycle-share index 276.2 (May 1896) → 65.6 (end-1898), –76.3% ✅. "70% failed" = 113 of 134 not in original form by 1910 ✅; strict bankruptcy 43/141 (~30%); reconstructions at 5–11% of par (Raleigh, Singer, Swift, Humber, Rover).
- **Route:** *same-market output overbuild* → **consolidation** (BSA, Raleigh, Hercules = 70% of employment by the mid-1930s). Exactly the framework's exclusion region.
- **Third claim:** **Hendee/Indian** — bought his insolvent firm's tools at auction (1897–98) ✅, sold bicycles at under half competitors' prices, then motorcycles (~1901).
- **Diffusion (cross-market transfer of *know-how*, not distressed plant):** Coventry cycle firms into cars and motorcycles (Rover, Humber, Singer, Swift, Riley, Triumph — the same owners redeploying living works); Dunlop/Palmer into motor tyres; Leland & Faulconer's chainless-bicycle bevel-gear plant → Cadillac rear axles by 1902; Baldwin Chain → automobile chains (1900); Coventry machine-tool makers (Alfred Herbert); Wright Cycle Company → aeroplane (shop skill + seasonal cash flow); cheaper bicycles for commuters and the children's market.
- **Rejected/thin:** Good Roads Movement (an 1880s lobby, before the mania; its macadam was destroyed by cars in the 1910s); Daimler's first Coventry works was a **cotton** mill, not a cycle shed; tube companies were destroyed (Accles 3% of par).
- **Scarce complement:** machine-tool designs and toolmakers (captured the gain), engine design and marques (Triumph, Indian), patents (Dunlop — but its 1896 balance sheet was 81% intangibles).
- **Lesson:** where the overbuild is *output capacity*, the transferable residue is **tooling and skills (the human/tool substrate)** — the C7 "recombination" channel — not the factories.

## H08 — The 1920s radio boom and RCA → `H08_Radio.md`

- **Built:** mostly **receiver** capacity (apparatus output $10.6m in 1921 → $411.6m in 1929; 4.4m home sets at ~$133 in 1929) plus scattered cheap stations; NBC+CBS tangible broadcast plant only **$9.3m** at end-1938 ✅. AT&T kept the long-distance wires.
- **RCA:** 5.825 (1921) → 420 (1928) → 5-for-1 split → 114.75 (Sep 1929) → 2.625 (May 1932), –98% ✅. Rise ≈9,750%, trough 1932 not 1929. Advertising model was AT&T's WEAF "toll broadcasting" (28 Aug 1922) ✅, not RCA's.
- **Metered input:** the sponsored clock-hour ($/hour, **daytime at half the evening card** ✅); evening card *rose* (WEAF $480–600 → $1,200 mid-1930s); cost per listener-home fell because household penetration went <1% (1922) → 46% (1930).
- **Route:** *same-market output overbuild* in receivers → consolidation and dealer collapse (set price $133 → $34, 1929–33; specialist dealers –26%) — the Case Library's verdict holds for this part. In broadcasting the cheapness came from **unsold inventory** (sustaining time >⅔ of national hours in 1931), not a write-down.
- **Third claims (new vs the Case Library):** (1) **daytime serials** — P&G/Oxydol's *Ma Perkins* (1933), the Hummerts, Irna Phillips; ~90% of sponsored daytime hours by 1940; (2) **disc syndication** — Gosden & Correll's *Amos 'n' Andy* (1928) recorded once and leased to stations, **bypassing AT&T's line charge** (the input that was never written down) → the transcription/syndication industry.
- **Diffusion:** national evening advertisers (American Tobacco, Pepsodent) and their agencies (Lord & Thomas ~30% of radio ad dollars 1927–31; JWT, Ayer, Rankin), network stars, baseball clubs (+12% attendance, no significant profit gain), the Crossley ratings service, radio repair, political broadcasting.
- **Unclear/excluded:** ASCAP (holds the copyrights — a toll on the medium, ≈ scarce complement), Philco/Emerson midget sets (same-market survivors), TV networks (same operators changing medium).
- **Scarce complement:** licences and clear channels (regulatory), affiliate contracts, AT&T's wire, the sponsor's trademark, characters and performer contracts, copyrights.
- **Lesson:** (a) a third claim can come from **off-peak / unsold inventory** priced below the peak card even when nothing is written down; (b) **bypassing the one input that stays dear** (the wire) is itself a business.

## H09 — Electricity: the Insull holding-company frenzy (1922–32) and cheap-power industries → `H09_Electricity.md`

- **Built:** operating utilities' generation, distribution and interconnection (net generation 39.4bn kWh 1920 → 92.2bn 1929 → 79.4bn 1932); the frenzy was the **holding-company layer** on top (Insull Utility Investments: $66m of debentures against ~$1.5m of unpledged assets in April 1932).
- **Metered input:** average revenue per kWh by class. **It rose through the crash**: all services 2.57¢ (1929) → 2.85¢ (1932); industrial 1.38¢ → 1.53¢; residential kept its long glide (16.2¢ 1902 → 6.33¢ 1929 → 5.60¢ 1932 → 3.84¢ 1940).
- **Route:** *securities write-down with no change in the operating asset's price* — Middle West and IUI receiverships (Apr 1932), common worthless; Commonwealth Edison etc. stayed solvent and paid dividends; PUHCA 1935 stripped write-ups. No third claim can form when the metered price does not fall.
- **Third claims:** none from the Insull crash. The only cheap-power third claims came via a **different route — long-term discriminatory contracts and public cost-based rates**: Alcoa at Niagara (~1.33 mills/kWh vs 3 mills for other customers, from 1895 — phrase located, sentence unverified), Alcoa at Bonneville (2 mills/kWh, 1938–39 ✅, vs a 10.6-mill 1940 US industrial average), TVA–Alcoa (1935), Oak Ridge (a state load).
- **Diffusion:** manufacturing on unit drive (David: ~half the 1919–29 TFP acceleration — *before* the crash), industrial purchased power (65% of motor hp by 1939 ✅), refrigerators (10% → 56% of households 1930–40), process-heat industries, electrified railroads, commercial air conditioning (load-balancing by 1941), rural co-ops (institutional — federal credit, 1936).
- **Rejected:** streetcars/suburbs/trolley parks (a declining load by 1927–42), elevators (a 1912-census load), Birdseye/King Kullen (not shown to depend on electricity prices).
- **Scarce complement:** the utility franchise and regulated rate base; for aluminium, **the power contract and the site** (Niagara Mohawk paid Alcoa $1.5m in 1949 to escape it — unverified).
- **Lesson:** (a) a securities collapse above a regulated asset base passes **nothing** downstream; (b) the cheap-power industries were the *intended* customers of purpose-built hydro on long contracts — the relevant AI analogue is the **data-centre PPA and large-load contract**, not a GPU fire-sale.

## H11 — Rubber: Amazonian wild rubber and the 1909–10 plantation share mania → `H11_Rubber.md`

- **Built:** Hevea plantations in Ceylon, Malaya, the Dutch East Indies (Malay States 542,877 acres by end-1911; ~4m acres "Middle East" by 1925); UK-registered rubber companies 1907–11 >£90m nominal, ~£60m paid up. Trees yield only after 6–8 years (sunk biological substrate ✅).
- **Metered input:** crude rubber per lb — 12s 9d (Apr 1910 peak) → 2s 4d (1914) → 9d (1921) → Stevenson spike 3s 9d (Jul 1925) → 2⅜¢/lb (1932).
- **P\*:** 1914 view — production "will not pay … at two shillings a pound"; Stevenson pivot 1s 6d.
- **Route:** *over-planting into a same-market glut* — the estates were built for tyre demand (80% of US rubber went into tyres in 1925 ✅); share write-downs (Shanghai index 240 → 15% of Jan 1910 level; London ~halved), trees stayed with the planting companies; then **cartel re-pricing** (Stevenson 1922–28) and IRRC (1934). The synthesis reverses causation: tyre demand created the plantations, not vice versa.
- **Third claims — the "new form of the input" mechanism:** ammonia-preserved **liquid latex**, impossible from wild rubber smoked into balls, enabled dipped and foamed goods: **latex condoms** (Youngs' latex Trojan 1929, London Rubber's Durex 1932 — WIKIPEDIA-ONLY chronology), **Dunlopillo latex foam** (1929 — cars, cinemas, mattresses); Lastex elastic yarn (unclear).
- **Diffusion:** tyre makers (Big Four 91% of US auto tyres by 1936), mass-market cars, buses and trucks, rubber footwear, mechanical rubber goods, cable insulation, drug sundries, hard-rubber radio panels.
- **Excluded:** bicycle tyres (a *cause* of the boom), Firestone Liberia / Fordlândia (buyers becoming producers in response to the Stevenson spike — new first claims).
- **Scarce complement:** tyre brands and dealer networks; for latex goods, dip recipes, automated lines, brands (Trojan, Durex, Dunlopillo).
- **Lessons:** (a) **form, not just price** — the third claim used a new physical form the abundance made shippable; (b) **"cheap" can be cartelised back** — Hansard ✅: Dutch share 15% → 37% under the British restriction; (c) smallholders' exit option (stop tapping, grow rice) kept supply from clearing out.

Grid "death-spiral" risk. (M-KOPA pay-as-you-go: unclear — the scarce complement is M-Pesa.)

**Tested, not kept:** container shipping (ocean freight factor did *not* fall at adoption; Walmart's China sourcing is diffusion; Hummels: air freight, not ocean, fell an order of magnitude), DRAM 1984–85 (same-market → consolidation; Intel kept value by *exiting* the glut product and keeping the microprocessor), streetcars/interurbans (suburbs and trolley parks were the builders' own product — Chevy Chase), supertankers (floating storage dearer than land tanks; the residue stayed in the oil trade), US offices late 1980s (vacancy 4.9% → 18.9%; second-claim buyers of the same asset; driven by tax-law swings).

**Lessons:** (a) **asset reversal** — a stranded asset can serve the opposite flow (import → export); (b) the **cheap flow** (gas, ethane, cells, panels), not the stranded concrete, is often what third claims use; (c) **the surviving scarce complement in a compute bust is the energised megawatt + interconnection**, not the chips — the direct precedent for an AI bust.

## H13 — Agri-biological manias (merino 1807–15; Morus multicaulis 1830s–39; hen fever 1849–55) and tulips (1636–37) → `H13_Agri_Bio_Tulips.md`

- **Metered input = the organism, not the fibre:** merino rams $300–1,000 (1810–11); mulberry trees $1–1.25 → 2–4¢ within 1839 ✅; fancy fowl ~$120/pair → unsaleable by 1855; tulip futures (Switsers 0.035 → 0.17 → 0.11 guilders/aas, Jan–Feb 1637).
- **Route:** *same-market output overbuild of a living, consumable stock* — sheep went to the butcher, trees were burned and then blighted (1844 ✅), fowl were eaten. **"A write-down that destroys the asset cannot cheapen a later user's input."**
- **Third claim (the only one):** **Vermont dairy** — the written-down asset was the *cleared hill pasture*, not the sheep: wool at 25¢/lb vs $1–2/yr keep; "the decline of the sheep industry in the late 1840s forced farmers to find alternative agricultural pursuits" ✅; enabled by the railway and the 1854 iced butter car; cows +85% 1850–1900, sheep 1.68m (1840) → 0.30m (1900).
- **Rejected from the synthesis:** merino capital funding the mills (mills predate the mania; tariff, not sheep, sustained them; Vermont mills 33 → ~100, 1836–50 ✅); hen fever → broiler revolution (broilers came from 1923 Delmarva, 1945–48 hybrid contests and integration — no link); multicaulis → nursery trade and farm press (the leading nursery, Prince, was a *casualty*; the silk journals died with the trees); **tulips as a "no lasting utility" control group — WRONG** (no serious economic distress per Garber, unverified here; production unchanged per Goldgar; the bulb trade continued).
- **Unclear:** Cheney Brothers (founded in the craze but pivoted *away* from the cheap input to imported Asian raw silk + thread machinery + tariff); the Spencer carbine from the Cheney tool room (a skills spin-off, not a cheap-input user); Vermont stud rams re-priced to $3,500 ✅ (the crash socialised the breed, then re-concentrated the rent in the pedigree).
- **Lesson:** the substrate that transferred was **land**, the most durable thing on the farm — consistent with C4 (substrate-to-overlay).

## H06 — Printing: the 1450s–1470s glut and the steam / penny press (1814–1840s) → `H06_Printing.md`

- **Built:** movable-type shops — ~205 European cities with a press by 1500; 27,873 editions before 1501; Venice ~200 firms. Overproduction crisis 1472 (Sweynheym & Pannartz: 12,475 volumes, many unsold; Venice glut). 19th c.: Koenig & Bauer steam cylinder press at *The Times* (1814, 1,100 sheets/hr); Applegath & Cowper (1828, >4,000/hr); **Hoe's rotary press only 1843–47** → the synthesis's "1820s rotary-press speculation" is **WRONG**; the Panic of 1825 was Latin American bonds and mining — publishing (Constable, Ballantyne, Scott's £121k) was a casualty of bills, not presses.
- **Metered input:** price of a book — fell ≥65% 1450–1500 (fact ✅, Grok's quote spliced); newspaper copy 6¢ → 1¢ (*New York Sun*, 1833); in Britain the floor was the **stamp tax** (4d of *The Times*'s 6d in 1815; cut to 1d in 1836).
- **Route:** exit and consolidation of printers (same market: books for readers) + **regulatory re-scarcity** (Venice 1517 decree cancelled privileges, new ones only for new works) + **R4** (tax cuts set the newspaper price floor).
- **Third claims (non-book customers):** merchants using printed **business-education manuals** (Dittmar & Seabold: business-education content ↔ city growth); **Amsterdam printed price current** (2 stuivers/copy ✅; 350 goods by 1635) — small firms got big-firm information; **Reformation pamphleteers** (cities with a press by 1500: +52.1 pp Protestant by 1530 — unverified); **shipmasters using printed sea atlases** (Waghenaer 1584); **corantos** (≥10 weekly in Amsterdam before 1645); **Holloway's pills** (advertising as the distribution system: £5k (1842) → ~£50k/yr); **penny-paper advertisers**.
- **Diffusion:** technical manuals (Biringuccio, Digges), Penny Magazine (200k circulation; died when price rose — same-market signature), Scott's Magnum Opus.
- **Scarce complement:** the trading seat and the Bourse (Amsterdam), pulpit/council/prince (Reformation), ship and cargo (atlases), **brand and secret recipe** (Holloway), a correspondent on the spot (corantos).
- **New mechanism — codified secrets:** cheap print **destroyed scarce know-how** (compound-interest tables "kept hidden … as great secrets" ✅, printed by Stevin 1582) — the gain went to ordinary merchants; and where the secret *was* the asset, cheap print was **refused** (VOC kept East-Indies charts hand-drawn for 150 years).
- **Lesson:** cheapness of codified knowledge redistributes value from **holders of secrets** to **holders of position** (seat, cargo, pulpit, brand). The closest historic analogue to cheap synthetic cognition.
