QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was metal-hulled steam tonnage, bunker stations, and the Suez cut, replacing sail on most routes to Britain. On the UK register, end-1881 tonnage was 6,691,996 tons, of which sail was 3,688,008 tons and steam 3,003,988 tons. End-1912 tonnage was 11,894,791 tons, of which sail was 902,718 tons and steam 10,992,073 tons. At the canal’s opening, about 80 percent of world shipping was still sail. In 1909 a Royal Commission put tramps at 70–80 percent of tonnage; tramps carried the bulk staples. The metered input downstream users bought was the tramp freight quotation in shillings and pence per ton of a named bulk cargo on a named route (Angier’s charter figures), not a pure ton-mile price. Meat had its own quotation: Argentine beef, pounds sterling per ton. The long-run price owners needed, P*, is the average total cost of the round voyage. In the cost accounts used for 1869–1913, capital (interest, depreciation and repairs) is 40 percent of voyage cost, coal 20, labour 10, port charges 25, other 5. No prospectus hurdle rate was recovered. From 1871/73 to 1887/89, falling factor prices explain 50–65 percent of the freight fall, of which cheaper ships (cheap iron hulls and cheaper shipbuilding) are 25–35 percent, and shipping productivity the remaining 35–50 percent. So the secular fall is a lower P* for the new ship, not a write-down of that ship. Cyclical rates did go below what the existing fleet had just been earning: see B6. The share of tonnage laid up is not in these pages.

Unprotected European grain producers, above all British agriculture, faced the grain invasion; the Liverpool–Chicago grain gap fell from 60.2 percent in 1870 to 14.2 percent in 1912, and the meat-and-fats gap from 93 percent to 18 percent. Continental states that could do so raised tariffs. US beef exporters lost the British market to Argentina after 1905–08. In New Zealand, boiling-down works were converted: Nelson’s Tomoana, opened in 1880 for tallow and canned meat, was built so it could switch to freezing. British fresh-beef prices stayed above Argentine chilled prices, but the import set the competitive pressure.

The 1901 drop, 29 to 34 percent in one year, and the 1884–86 trough, were crashes relative to the previous boom. The long-run level was also a lower cost of the new ship, so “below P*” is clear for the cycle and not automatically clear for every new steel steamer. The people who used the trough were the charterers of bulk tramp cargoes, grain, coal, timber and ore. The pages do not name a merchant house as the residual claimant. QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: The Dunedin, a sailing ship fitted with a Bell-Coleman plant, left Port Chalmers on 15 February 1882. The meat sold in London within a fortnight for more than twice the New Zealand price. The trade was not consistently profitable until the mid-1890s, then reached 5.8 million sheep carcasses in 1910. Early value sat with the colonial seller and with works such as Nelson Brothers; by the 1930s British retailers, Vestey among them, owned many of the works. Argentina: River Plate Fresh Meat froze beef from 1884 and sent the first chilled shipment in 1902. Chilled beef from Argentina was almost 500,000 quarters in 1906 and 1907, nearly a quarter of UK meat imports on the page’s wording. Freight on Argentine beef fell from about £25 per ton in 1870 to about £5 per ton in 1912. Who kept the value changed with the quota fights. Packers needed refrigerated tonnage; US firms held 58.50 percent of the reserved refrigerated allocation in the 1914 agreement. London prices of Argentine chilled beef stayed below English beef, so consumers gained, while cattle prices in Argentina spiked when packers competed for stock, and in 1913 British packers said chilled beef could not be shipped at a profit. Vestey’s capture is real and late: retail shops, cold stores, and plants bought or built from the war into the 1920s. Bananas paid because the fruit could not be grown in the consuming country and, once a fast owned steamer existed, could be sold year-round. Baker’s 1870 margin was a shilling in Jamaica against $2 a bunch in Jersey City. United Fruit, from 1899, held the plantations, the ships and the US distribution, and bought Elders and Fyffes. The scarce piece was not cheap tramp space. It was speed, cold, and the vertical chain.

PART C

1. The secular freight fall was a new, lower cost of the new ship, so it was a new P*, not a write-down of the builders’ capital. Falling ship prices and productivity, not a distress sale, dominate the 1871/73–1887/89 accounting. Third-claim gains, in the strict sense, are the cycle troughs of the mid-1880s and 1901–08, taken by whoever was chartering that year. They did not found a permanent new industry. Most accounts that start from “cheap steam made global business” collapse those two mechanisms into one.
SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9531/w9531.pdf
QUOTE: "Falling ship prices, driven by the introduction of cheap iron hulls and productivity gains in the shipbuilding industry, resulted in around 25-35% of the fall in freight rates."

2. Outward coal freight was cheap because of joint production, even when the round voyage only covered cost. Britain’s largest export by tons was the backhaul of the grain and ore trades. It looked, at the time, like a coal boom. It was also a pricing identity of tramp shipping.
SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9531/w9531.pdf
QUOTE: "the sum of the freight rates on the different legs will still equal the sum of marginal costs."

3. The first long-distance frozen meat from New Zealand moved on a sailing ship, and the first chilled beef into Britain was American. From 1875 to 1889 the United States supplied 93 percent of British fresh beef. Steam and Suez were not the binding constraint for either start. Refrigerated cubic capacity was scarce and paid a premium, which is why packers and retailers, not general tramp owners, ended up with the chain.
SOURCE: https://nzhistory.govt.nz/first-shipment-of-frozen-meat-leaves-nz
QUOTE: "Davidson decided to fit out a passenger sailing ship, the Albion Line’s Dunedin, with a coal-powered Bell Coleman freezing plant"

4. Scheduled and perishable trades did not buy the glut. Refrigerated meat to Britain was rationed by a freight committee because boat space, not the tramp rate, was the constraint. United Fruit owned its fleet. The cheap Isserlis index is a bulk-tramp index. Liner rates were a different market.
SOURCE: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/revista-de-historia-economica-journal-of-iberian-and-latin-american-economic-history/article/south-american-meat-industry-during-the-first-global-economy-18601930/08950A0CBAEF67746C079B19798424EB
QUOTE: "this arrangement was not secret and was countenanced by British law"

5. Two negative results that the “steam integrated the world” sentence misses. Instrumented freight does not explain the 1870–1913 trade-volume boom; income growth does. And sail was still competitive in 1914 on Australian routes, California wheat, and west-coast nitrates. The same technology that created new bulk trades left a set of long routes inside the old sail market. Pascali’s further cut is institutional: steam changed trade patterns, and only a small number of countries with more inclusive institutions gained from the integration.
SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14139/w14139.pdf
QUOTE: "we find no evidence that the maritime transport revolution was the primary driver of the late nineteenth century global trade boom."
