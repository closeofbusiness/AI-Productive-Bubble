Metered input. The inland freight rate, in shillings per ton-mile: about 0.15s in current prices around 1845 (0.09s in 1700 prices) and about 0.10s around 1865 (0.06s in 1700 prices), from Hawke via Bogart. P*. Investors hoped for a 10 per cent profit. The 1845 parliamentary plans, as Odlyzko summarises them, averaged about £15,600 construction cost per mile, £1,670 revenue per mile, working expenses at 40 per cent of revenue, and a 7 per cent dividend. A 10 per cent return on a railway costing about £23,000 a mile, at a one-third working-expense ratio, required about £3,000 of revenue per mile. Actual 1850 figures were £35,200 of capital per mile and £2,080 of revenue per mile. Common-share dividends averaged 1.9 per cent of paid-up capital in 1849 and 2.8 per cent over 1849–58. On the York and North Midland, pre-1845 lines were earning 7.65 per cent after debt interest, 1845 lines 4.15 per cent, and 1846 lines 0.07 per cent.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: The second claim stayed largely with the original ordinary shareholders. Odlyzko’s conclusion is that parliamentary limits on leverage produced very few bankruptcies, and that lenders and preference shareholders were not the losers. Shareholders met calls through the crash: a 10 per cent deposit (raised again in July 1845) left most of the share uncalled, and the Economist described holders selling the called shares, or other shares, to find the cash. Both parliamentary commercial-distress committees treated railway calls as a cause of the 1847 crisis. Market prices did the writing-down: about 60 per cent off the 1845 peak by 1849–50, while book capital stayed near £35,000 a mile. Control then moved by amalgamation. Campbell finds established companies buying newer, lower-return lines. The East Anglian, sanctioned in 1845, cost about £18,342 a mile against an estimate of £8,786, earned £687 per mile in 1855 against £1,070 promised, paid no common dividend, and passed into the Great Eastern in 1862. The 1844 Act’s option for the state to buy later railways was not used. There was no general sale of the network at a single fraction of cost.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: The short-lived part was the iron rail, the sleepers and the rolling stock. Iron-rail life on heavy fast lines was about three and a half years; at Camden in the early 1860s an iron rail face could be gone in about two months. QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: Long-distance stagecoach operators and the turnpike interest lost the passenger. Around 1865 a third-class railway fare was about a quarter of the stagecoach fare of 1800, and railway journey speed was about 23 miles per hour against about 8 for the coach in the 1820s. Canal freight, about 0.20s per ton-mile around 1840, was matched by rail in the 1840s and undercut by rail’s 0.10s around 1865; canals kept some bulk traffic but lost the high-value and passenger trade. Coastal passenger steam on parallel routes was cut: Bogart cites the London–Edinburgh second-class steamship fare falling from £2 10s to £1 15s when the railway link opened. Urban cowkeepers lost London’s milk: probably over 20,000 cows within eight or nine miles of the Royal Exchange in the early 1850s, and town plus road milk no more than 4 per cent of London’s supply by 1914. Mail-coach contractors lost the letter to the 1838 statutory rail carriage.

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: Odlyzko: railway construction investment was £13 million in 1845, £30 million in 1846 and £44 million in 1847, almost 8 per cent of British GDP in 1847 and twice that year’s military budget, returning to £13 million a year only in 1850. The gap is the estimator and the national-income denominator; both series agree that 1847 was the peak and that the share was several per cent of GDP. The call mechanism: shares were partly paid. QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: Freight rates did fall, by about one-third in nominal terms: from about 0.15s per ton-mile around 1845 to about 0.10s around 1865 (Hawke via Bogart). In 1700 prices the same movement is 0.09s to 0.06s. Passenger fares around 1865 were about 0.13s per mile second class and 0.08s third class. The penny ceiling is earlier than the crash. The 1844 Act required one train each way daily, at not less than 12 miles per hour, stopping at every station, at not more than 1d per mile for third class. That was written when the policy fear was profits above 10 per cent, and the purchase option in the same Act was not used. Competition from duplicate lines and statutory maxima both held rates down. The capital write-down showed up more in the return than in the tariff: revenue per mile fell from £3,470 in 1845 to £2,080 in 1850 while capital per mile stayed about £35,000, and ordinary dividends fell to about 2–3 per cent. Book capital was generally not written off; shareholders simply earned less on it. Marginal traffics such as milk were then priced as poorly paying fill-in business on that sunk network.
SOURCE: https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dbogart/transport_revolution_surveyjan2013.pdf
QUOTE: "Rail rates decreased even more by 1865."

What a later user inherited was a formation and a right-of-way whose original rail had already been replaced several times. 4. The penny train is a boom-era regulation, not a gift of the bust. It was imposed in 1844, with a state purchase option aimed at railways earning more than 10 per cent. Cheap third class was therefore inside the builders’ political bargain. Businesses that needed only a penny a mile and a fast train (Cook’s 1841 shilling excursion; Smith’s newspapers) are diffusion. The clearer third-claim cases are traffics the companies carried while calling them unremunerative, above all long-distance milk after the 1865–66 rate cut. SOURCE: https://www.historyhome.co.uk/peel/railways/railways.htm QUOTE: "charging no more than 1d. per mile for third class passengers."

5. There was no clean hand-over to a distressed buyer. Ordinary shareholders paid the calls after prices had broken, kept the lines, and earned about 2–3 per cent. Preference capital and debt were protected. The accounting profession and the Clearing House grew up beside that struggle. They are consequences of joint-stock railways and of bad accounts, not industries whose input was a cheap ton-mile. Deloitte’s 1849 Great Western appointment belongs with the shareholder investigations, not with Bass or the dairies.
