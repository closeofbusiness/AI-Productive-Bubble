PART B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was a stock of Hevea brasiliensis, plus estate roads, smokehouses and labour lines, in Ceylon, Malaya and the Dutch East Indies. Malay States acreage was 542,877 acres at the end of 1911. A January 1913 consular figure gave Ceylon 220,000 acres and the Malay States 430,000 acres in 1912, plus over 100,000 acres elsewhere. By 1925 about 4 million acres in the Middle East were under rubber, about 70 percent of that acreage in British territory. World raw-rubber output in one later compilation was 90,500 tonnes in 1910, 120,400 tonnes in 1914, 370,000 tonnes in 1920 and 707,000 tonnes in 1932. Plantation share of that total is given as 9.1 percent in 1910, 59.3 percent in 1914, 88.7 percent in 1920 and 93.4 percent in 1922. Trees took 6 to 8 years to yield, so 1905–11 plantings matured about 1912–19.
Metered input: the London price of crude rubber per pound (fine hard Para, or plantation sheet and crepe, which traded at a discount to Para). New York cents per pound are the US quotation of the same commodity.
P*: in 1914, informed opinion held that production would not pay at 2s. per lb unless costs fell. Favoured Ceylon estates then produced at 6d. per lb; the expected average once all trees yielded was 8d. per lb; the 1911 cost was given as 1s. 4d. per lb. The Stevenson Committee’s explicit pivot, from 1922, was 1s. 6d. per lb, described as a satisfactory profit and enough to induce new planting. A 1909–10 prospectus selling-price assumption was not on the pages fetched.
Price path: April 1910 spot peak 12s. 9d. per lb for fine hard Para (another 1914 account says 12s. 6d.). The 1910 yearly average is 8s. 9d. per lb in one series and 7s. 7½d. per lb in the Colonial Secretary’s 1925 figure. By 1914 the yearly average was 2s. 4d. per lb. The 1921 London average was 9d. per lb, and the US price went as low as 14 cents per lb. In 1932 rubber sold as low as 2⅜ cents per lb. That April 1910 peak of 12s. 9d. is 153 pence per lb; 9d. in 1921 is about 6 percent of that peak.
Idle capacity: in 1914 a large share of the acreage was immature, hence not yet tappable, rather than abandoned. In 1919, 1920 and 1921 production exceeded consumption and stocks piled up. From 1 November 1922 the Stevenson duty let growers export only 60 percent of “standard production” (output in the year beginning 1 November 1919) at the minimum duty; the quota later fell to 50 percent. That is at least 40 to 50 percent of 1919–20 output shut in, and a larger share of 1925 potential, because acreage had kept maturing. A parliamentary question put the cut at 50,500 tons per year; the minister did not confirm that tonnage. In 1932, with the price below the cost of production, some smallholders stopped tapping. At Labuhan Bilik, smallholder rubber exports fell from about 4,132 tons in 1929 to 1,811 tons in 1932.

BENEFICIARY: Latex condom makers (Youngs Rubber’s latex Trojan; London Rubber Company’s Durex)
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: Rubber condoms from 1855; cement-dipping (dry rubber dissolved in gasoline or benzene) commercialised by Julius Fromm in 1912. London Rubber, previously a wholesaler of German condoms, began making Durex in 1932. Worldwide condom sales doubled in the 1920s. QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: The trees mostly stayed with the companies that had planted them. The write-down was a collapse in the price of their shares and in the price of rubber, not a receivership that handed the estates to a new industry.
On the Shanghai Stock Exchange, a weighted index of 48 rubber stocks reached 240 percent of its January 1910 level in March 1910, then 27 percent of the January level in July and 15 percent in September. From the March peak to the September level, that is 15/240 of the peak, or about 6 percent. London was milder. A 43-company London index reached 143 percent of its January 1910 level in April and was at 77 percent of the January level by December 1910, about half of the April peak. Only 19 of the 48 Shanghai companies had visible rubber output in 1910. Some promotions were false. The estates that were real, such as Tebong, kept planting, paid dividends and, when equity finance dried up, issued debentures (Tebong sought 250,000 taels in 1913).
By 1914, boom capitalisation was £54 to £76 per acre against a development cost of £20 to £30 per acre, and an Economist figure of £40,000,000 had been invested at boom prices. There is no fetched series for the discount of market value to paid-up capital in the 1921 or 1932 troughs.
From 1 November 1922 the same owners faced an export quota, initially 60 percent of standard production, later 50 percent, enforced by a prohibitive duty above the quota. That is withheld output, not a change of owner. The minister in charge said the scheme restricted export at the low duty, and that its object was to preserve the industry. Dutch supplies, outside the scheme, rose from 15 percent to 37 percent of the trade. The International Rubber Regulation Committee of June 1934 again set quotas for producers of 98 percent of world supply, still the existing plantation industry.
A partial transfer ran the other way: manufacturers bought some acreage. By 1936 U.S. Rubber owned 76,563 acres in Sumatra and Malaya. That was integration by a consumer, and it covered a quarter of one firm’s needs, not a purchase of the written-down stock at a known fraction of cost.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: The durable piece was biological and civil, not a cable in the ground. Hevea was unfit to tap until about seven years old, and the bark was renewed on a cycle of about four years of thin daily parings. Once the land was cleared, the trees planted and the labour settled, those costs were sunk and could not be shifted to another crop quickly. Estate buildings and machinery were real but secondary: in one wartime year Tebong spent 83,575 taels of an 86,800-tael profit on new buildings and machinery.
What survived into the 1920s and 1930s was the planted acreage itself. The 1905–11 trees were in their bearing years when tyre consumption and the later latex trades grew. Short-lived inputs were tapping labour, cups, knives and the latex, which had to be coagulated or preserved within days. Under Stevenson, a large share of potential latex was left in the tree by quota. In the 1932 price trough, some smallholders in East Sumatra stopped tapping and went back to rice, while the trees remained. World output nevertheless stayed high: 707,000 tonnes in 1932 against 90,500 tonnes in 1910.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The displaced incumbents were the high-cost wild-rubber suppliers.
Brazil had supplied almost 90 percent of world rubber exports around the turn of the century. Brazilian output was still about 42,000 tons around 1909–12, while plantation output rose from about 4,000 tons in 1909 to 30,000 tons in 1912. A later compilation shows Brazilian wild rubber at 53,200 tonnes in 1910, 33,500 tonnes in 1914 and 18,500 tonnes in 1920, and Brazil’s share of world rubber at 1.6 percent by 1939. The cost of Para rubber, including export tax, was put at 72 cents per lb, of which the tax was 24 cents, against plantation costs of 6d. to 1s. 4d. per lb. The commercial economies of Manaus and Belém lost the export trade that had supported them.
African wild rubber, mostly Landolphia vines that were cut rather than tapped, fell with it. The Congo’s share of world production went from 11 percent in 1900 to 2 percent in 1914. Congo exports in the same compilation peak at 6,500 tonnes in 1904 and are 2,500 tonnes in 1914 and 1,000 tonnes in 1920.
The Peruvian Amazon Company was in voluntary liquidation by July 1912.
Among manufacturers, the pressure showed up as consolidation and thinner margins, not as the death of a previous material. Longer-lived tyres, retreading and better casings reduced rubber used per mile and flattened demand that planters had treated as limitless. Reclaimed rubber is mentioned as part of 1912 supply. A later share series showing reclaim losing ground when virgin rubber was cheap was not on the pages fetched.

QUESTION: B7

Companies registered in Great Britain between 1907 and 1911 had nominal capital above £90,000,000, of which about £60,000,000 was paid up. Another contemporary figure is £40,000,000 invested at boom prices. Twenty-seven rubber companies raised new capital on the London Stock Exchange in 1906 alone, before the climax. London share prices in a 43-company sample rose from the January 1910 base to 143 percent in April and ended the year at 77 percent of January. That is a break of roughly half from the spring peak, not a wipe-out.
Shanghai was sharper and smaller. A student essay, citing a Chinese article citing The Times, says 122 rubber companies were set up from late 1909 to early 1910, about 40 of them in Shanghai, and that Shanghai issues raised 20 to 30 million taels. A cleaner account of the exchange itself: in the first six months of 1910, thirty rubber estates raised 13.5 million taels; 48 rubber stocks were quoted in taels; only 19 had visible output that year; the index went to 240 percent of January by March and to 15 percent of January by September. Chinese investors were later estimated to have lost 20 million taels. Between mid-1910 and early 1911, an estimate in the same research puts 53 to 70 percent of Shanghai native banks in failure. Foreign banks then lent about 8.5 million taels into the money-market rescue, against about 2.8 million taels of domestic rescue funds. The Shanghai rubber cohort’s market value in 1910 was no more than one-third of the London rubber cohort’s, and the whole Shanghai list was about 120 stocks against 1,198 in London.
Malayan finance, in the sources fetched, was layered. Early money came from proprietors of Ceylon tea estates. The acceleration was new equity in London, supplemented by Shanghai listings promoted by trading houses such as Jardine, Matheson and David Sassoon. Buyers often paid about a quarter up front. Local “dollar-company” capital existed but was the smaller pool. After the break, going concerns financed themselves by retained profits and by debentures: Tebong in 1915 applied 83,575 taels of an 86,800-tael profit to capital account, and in 1913 it had gone to the debenture market for working capital. J. H. Drabble’s book is the standard account of this mix and is cited by the historians used here. His own split between agency-house capital, London issues and retained earnings was not quoted on the pages fetched.

PART C

1. The draft reverses the causation. Tyre demand drove the rubber price to its 1910 peak and thereby called the plantations into being. Cheap rubber was the residue of that overplanting. It was not a prior condition without which the mass-market car could not exist. Firestone’s own formulation was that without rubber there could be no tyres and without tyres no automobiles, which is an argument for planting, written from the tyre factory outward.
SOURCE: https://eh.net/encyclopedia/the-international-natural-rubber-market-1870-1930/
QUOTE: "led first to a spike in rubber prices; second, it led to the development of rubber plantations in Asia."

2. Because the estates were built for the existing rubber-goods market, the residue was more of the same input. That predicts consolidation and lower profits among the buyers, which is what happened. In 1936 four US firms made 91 percent of automobile tyres. Tread life rose from about 8,000 miles to 15,000 and then toward 20,000, so each car consumed less rubber per mile. Planters and tyre makers both lost the scarcity rent. Motorists received it.

QUOTE: "Rubber’s Big Four annually make 91% of all the tires for U.S. automobiles."

3. The input that was genuinely new was not only a lower price per pound of dry rubber. It was ammonia-preserved liquid latex, which a wild-rubber trade that smoked latex into balls on the river could not ship to a dipping shop. Hopkinson’s process around 1920, the latex Trojan of 1929, Durex in 1932, and Murphy’s foam in 1929 are the businesses that used that form. Contemporaries in 1914 could see a glut below 2s. per lb and could not see the use that would absorb it.

4. “Cheap” was a policy variable. With the pivot set at 1s. 6d. per lb, quotas of 50 to 60 percent of standard production were enough, once stocks were gone, to put the London price at 3s. 9d. per lb on 8 July 1925 and the US price near $1.15 to $1.21 per lb. Tyre prices and, on the manufacturers’ account, the whole list of rubber goods were marked up. Dutch producers, outside the scheme, increased their share from 15 percent to 37 percent. The minister confirmed that he was aware of that shift. The second claim did not lock up a wasted network. Unrestricted Dutch estates and smallholders expanded into the gap the British quota opened.

5. The capacity that kept price below the estates’ required return included producers the 1909–10 prospectuses did not control. East Sumatran smallholders planted Hevea from about 1910, gained from Stevenson because the Dutch East Indies were outside it, and were still expanding after the scheme ended in 1928. When the price fell to 0.31 guilders per kg in 1932 they could stop tapping and sell rice, areca and copra instead. That option is why trees were not scrapped and why supply did not exit cleanly at the sterling companies’ cost of production. The same logic made Firestone’s Liberia and the American Amazon experiments strategically loud and quantitatively small: 5 percent of Firestone’s needs, and a failed attempt to rebuild in Brazil a supply Asia already had.
