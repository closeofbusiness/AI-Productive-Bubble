QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: Control moved differently in each kept episode.

Shale molecules. The gas and ethane were not sold as a distressed asset to a new owner of the wells. The price fell, and the same producing sector kept selling, or left ethane in the gas. The write-down sat with producers’ reserves and equity. No page read here gives the cents on the dollar for a named producer bankruptcy. The ethylene plants and Cheniere’s liquefaction trains were new money, built because the molecule was cheap.

Sabine Pass. Same owners. Cheniere kept the terminal. At 31 March 2008 the firm had a stockholders’ deficit of $343 million, long-term debt of $2.757 billion, and was exploring a sale or other strategic options while cutting staff. No sale closed on the evidence read. In 2010 the same partnership started liquefaction on the existing tanks and berths. Creditors and equity of the import project absorbed the failed import thesis. The fraction of the $1.4 billion construction cost written off was not stated.

Containers. The $570 million, 12-ship order is sourced. Who received the ships, and at what fraction of the $570 million, was not on a page that returned text.

Cells. No distressed sale of a named factory was on the BloombergNEF page. The transfer is a price cut. Margins at the cell makers were squeezed, and volume moved to storage buyers and to foreign buyers willing to pay more than the Chinese price.

DRAM. Control left the US merchant suppliers. Intel, Motorola, National and AMD stopped 64K production. Mostek failed. Japanese firms were found, preliminarily, to be selling below cost, so they were not buying distressed US fabs in the page’s account. They kept producing at the low price.

Offices. The write-down hit lenders and then the federal resolution machinery. A sample of 224 nonperforming commercial-property loans in FDIC receiverships had lost 54 percent of assigned collateral value on average by 1992. Half the Connecticut loans in that sample had lost 63 percent or more, and half the Texas and Louisiana loans 58 percent or more. That is collateral versus original valuation, not a sale price versus construction cost. Vacant buildings stayed office buildings and were sold to new owners of the same asset. Those buyers are a second claim.

Tankers. Sanko’s creditors refused new loans and the firm filed in August 1985, with about $2 billion of bank debt and about $2 billion more claimed by trading and chartering counterparties. The ships were mostly chartered in from foreign owners rather than owned outright. A later sale price as a fraction of newbuild cost was not given. Time magazine’s 1975 account says producing countries were in a position to buy surplus ships cheaply. That would be a second claim buying the same ships.

Streetcars. Interurban securities failed early and often. The automobile did not buy the track and operate it as a cheap input. Chevy Chase shows the other pattern: the land company already owned both the railway and the lots.

Mining sites. Core Scientific’s equity absorbed a 98 percent share-price fall in the December 2022 Chapter 11. The reorganized firm kept the sites and leased 200 megawatts to CoreWeave. A $1 billion all-cash bid was reported in June 2024 and is not, on this page, a completed transfer.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: What the beneficiaries actually used was often a cheap flow, not the builders’ stranded concrete.

Shale. Wells and the molecules are short-lived. The price stayed low for years because drilling continued. The durable pieces that beneficiaries use are fractionators, ethane pipes, cracker vessels and, at Sabine Pass, tanks and berths built for import. Liquefaction trains are new. The Department of Energy’s cracker map is a map of new plants, not of reopened 2008 gas wells.

Containers. The durable pieces are ports, cranes and channels. Ships are wasting assets. New York longshore productivity more than doubled by 1975, but the money saving was delayed until the 1980s, when guaranteed-income payments shrank. The residue that reached shippers was a modestly lower freight factor plus faster, more reliable handling, not a permanent pile of written-off hulls.

Cells and solar panels. Factories are semi-durable. The product the downstream user buys is a flow of cheap packs or panels. The installed battery or rooftop array then lasts for years at the user’s site. Nameplate cell capacity at 2.5 times demand is factory overcapacity, not a stock of idle packs sitting in a warehouse on the page’s account.

DRAM. Fabs are durable, but the page shows firms exiting a product, not a later industry reusing those fabs. The chip itself was consumed in the computer.

Offices. The buildings are the long-lived substrate, and they survived. In 1994 only 1.7 million square feet were completed in the 31-market office set, so the 1980s stock was what remained. It remained offices. Vacancy of 18.9 percent in 1991 is the idle share.

Tankers. Hulls are mobile and were laid up, cancelled or sold. They are not a right-of-way. Floating storage was a use of the hull, and in 1977 it was still being compared with land storage rather than replacing it. Japan’s Ministry of International Trade and Industry put floating storage at about 7,000 yen per kiloliter, against about 5,500 yen for land storage.

Streetcars. Track and cars were abandoned as the automobile won. The review of Hilton and Due calls the industry a total financial failure with only a few lines left. A measured later reuse of rights-of-way was not on the pages read. The durable asset in the Chevy Chase case was the land, and the builder already owned it.

Mining campuses. Shells, substations and power contracts are the durable residue. Mining computers were moved to other mining sites. The GPUs are new.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The displaced incumbents, by episode.

Ethane cracking displaced naphtha and other liquids as the US ethylene feedstock. Ethane was 78 percent of 2019 US ethylene. US ethylene spot prices in 2017–2020 were 38–77 percent below European and Asian averages, which is the pressure on naphtha crackers. Chinese tariffs then hit US polyethylene, so the loss was not only foreign crackers. It was also US derivative exports into China. Gas producers lost the 2008 price. Import-terminal capacity holders lost the import thesis. Total and Chevron had Sabine Pass terminal-use agreements timed for 2009.

Container logistics, on the Frontline account, displaced US plants in socks, apparel, furniture, bicycles, electronics and similar goods. Kentucky Derby Hosiery is the named case: half of 1,500 US jobs were expected to go as production moved to meet Walmart’s price. Longshore gangs lost work. Their unions captured much of the early productivity gain, so the loss to labor was delayed into the 1980s.

Cheap cells displaced higher-cost chemistries and squeezed smaller cell makers. Storage did not, on the page, name a displaced incumbent other than more expensive ways of providing grid flexibility. That displacement is not quantified there.

Pakistani rooftop solar displaces grid kilowatt-hours and diesel pump fuel. The grid loses revenue. Households that cannot buy panels remain on the higher tariff.

DRAM’s losers were the US merchant memory firms named above. The computer industry was the buyer, not the displaced party. Magnetic-core memory had already been displaced by Intel’s 1103 in 1970, a decade before this glut.

Offices displaced nothing downstream. Developers, thrifts and banks absorbed the loss. Rents fell only from about $24 to about $20 per square foot.

Tankers displaced the voyage rents of 1973. Algerian and Libyan location premiums of as much as $2 per barrel were cut because freight no longer made nearby oil special. Owners such as Sanko, and yards holding 373 unfinished ships, were the losers. Oil buyers gained cheaper transport of the same cargo.

Streetcars lost to cars and trucks. That is a rival technology, not a customer of cheap traction current.

QUESTION: B7

Shale ethane. Metered input: dollars per gallon of ethane, from nearly $0.97 in October 2011 to $0.09 in March 2020, with Henry Hub gas from $8.86 per million Btu in 2008 to $2.03 in 2020. Sabine Pass. Metered input: Henry Hub dollars per million Btu, same path, plus a planned export fee of $1.40 to $1.75 per million Btu. Containers. Metered input: ad valorem ocean freight factor, down 2.4 percentage points from 1973 to 1988, not at adoption. United States Lines’ $570 million, 12-ship order is the sourced overbuild. PART C

1. Ocean container rates are the wrong collapse. Containers raised New York port labor productivity 136 percent from 1966 to 1975, and the trade-weighted freight factor did not fall while that happened. It fell 2.4 percentage points of import value only from 1973 to 1988. Hummels finds ocean’s ad valorem cost little changed from the 1950s, and air-freight costs down by an order of magnitude. Walmart’s China system is real. It is a poor illustration of a price falling below the shipowners’ required return, and Apple’s factories are a poor illustration of cheap boxes. High-value goods fit the air result. The container story was hard to see because the cranes arrived in the late 1960s and the freight factor moved only in the 1980s, after longshore guaranteed-income deals faded.
SOURCE: https://mail.freit.org/ETOS/papers/bridgman.pdf
QUOTE: "Freight rates did not fall in the initial containerization wave, coincident with the Kennedy Round."

2. Streetcar suburbs and trolley parks were the first claim. Francis Newlands’s Chevy Chase Land Company, founded 1890, bought control of the Rock Creek Railway, assembled more than 1,700 acres, opened the line in 1892, and operated the amusement park at the end of the line from 1894. The park existed to sell lots and to fill cars on evenings and weekends. Interurban finance, on the Hilton and Due review, lost money almost from the opening decade. The bust did not leave a cheap tracked input for a later industry. It left failed securities and, in this case, a suburb the builder had planned.
SOURCE: https://www.chevychasehistory.org/trolley-parks-and-real-estate-development
QUOTE: "it was operated by the Chevy Chase Land Company, the developer of the new planned suburb of Chevy Chase, Maryland."

3. Floating storage did not turn surplus tankers into a new industry. In March 1975 a Persian Gulf–Rotterdam spot voyage that had cost $8.8 million before the embargo was offered at $800,000, below amortization. By November 1977 Worldscale 22 left owners about $2,000 a day net on the spot voyage, and a storage charter looked better to the owner. Japan’s own ministry still put floating storage above land storage, about $22 a ton against about $17. The US strategic-reserve study rejected hulls because of hazard and cost versus salt caverns. Sanko’s 1985 failure, about $4 billion of claimed debts, was the write-down of an owner who had already tried to flee tankers for bulk carriers. The cheap residue stayed inside the oil trade.
SOURCE: https://time.com/archive/6850944/shipping-superbust/
QUOTE: "Today a 220,000-ton tanker can be spot-chartered for the Rotterdam run for as little as $800,000"

4. The office glut predicts consolidation and a second claim, and that is what it delivered. Vacancy in 31 major markets rose from 4.9 percent in 1980 to 18.9 percent in 1991. Rents in one national index fell only from about $24 to about $20 a square foot. Collateral on a sample of failed-bank commercial loans was down 54 percent. The buildings were still offices. No page documented a new industry that existed only because rents were below the 1981 tax-driven return. The non-obvious mechanism is the tax swing itself: a worked example in the FDIC history puts the after-tax return at 21.5 percent under the 1981 rules and 14.5 percent after 1986, which pulled capital in and then out without creating a downstream user.
SOURCE: https://www.fdic.gov/resources/publications/history-eighties/volume-1/history-80s-volume-1-part1-03.pdf
QUOTE: "nearly quadrupling between 1980 and 1991 from 4.9 percent to a peak of 18.9 percent."

5. Two busts that look like “the hardware was reused” were mostly a scarce complement sitting next to a cheap commodity. Core Scientific’s 2024 contracts put new Nvidia GPUs into old powered shells. The mining computers were sent to other mines. The scarcity that survived bankruptcy was the megawatt, not the ASIC. The DRAM bust is the older version of the same pattern. Chips fell from $3.50 to $0.35 and the US merchant industry exited. The firm that kept the value, Intel, stopped selling the cheap input and kept the microprocessor. Grove called that exit the best decision the company made. Contemporaries watching a memory glut would have predicted more and cheaper computers, which happened, and would have missed that the lasting firm was the one that abandoned the glut product.
SOURCE: https://timeline.intel.com/1985/farewell-to-dram
QUOTE: "a glut of supply for DRAM had irrevocably depressed the market by 1985, prompting Intel to exit"
