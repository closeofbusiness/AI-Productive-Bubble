Part B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was a receiver industry plus a scattered station system, tied together by AT&T wires, not a single tower network. Licensed stations rose from 28 to 570 during 1922. Sets in use were about 60,000 in 1922, 1,500,000 in 1923, and 5,000,000 homes by September 1926, when RCA said 21,000,000 homes remained unequipped. Households with a radio were under 1 percent in 1922, 16.0 percent in 1926, 45.8 percent in 1930, and 67.3 percent in 1935 (Scott and Walker, from Historical Statistics). A parallel Commerce series used by Surdam, Kanago, and Brown puts the share at about 0.2 percent in 1922, 10 percent in 1925, 35 percent in 1929, 46 percent in 1930, 60 percent in 1932, and 66 percent in 1935. Factory value of radio apparatus and tubes rose from 10,648,000 dollars in 1921 to 411,637,000 dollars in 1929. Retail value of all radio equipment was 842,500,000 dollars in 1929, of which home sets were 4,438,000 units at 592,000,000 dollars, or 133 dollars per set excluding tubes.

The metered input that advertisers bought was a sponsored hour, and fractions of an hour, on a named station or a wired chain. The unit is dollars per clock-hour, with daytime hours priced at one-half the evening card. It is not dollars per ton-mile and not the price of a receiver. WEAF’s own evening hour was 480 to 600 dollars in 1926; a ten-minute morning talk was 100 dollars and an evening ten-minute talk 200 dollars. At the end of 1925 AT&T sold a 13-station basic network at 2,600 dollars per hour and was grossing about 750,000 dollars a year from time. In September 1926 the full evening chain of listed stations was 4,080 dollars per hour, and daytime was half. By the mid-1930s NBC’s evening card ran from 120 dollars per hour on the smallest affiliates to 1,200 dollars for WEAF, WJZ, and WLW; CBS ran from 125 to 1,250 dollars per converted evening hour. Daytime remained one-half, and post-midnight one-third. Nominal card rates therefore rose. What fell was the cost per listener-home, because the denominator grew from well under 1 percent of households to roughly half by the early 1930s, and because most hours were not sold at the evening card at all.

P* for RCA, in the Sarnoff “Radio Music Box” text preserved by Gleason Archer, was about 75 dollars per receiver, with the main revenue from set sales: 100,000 sets “could yield a handsome profit,” and 1,000,000 families, 7 percent of about 15,000,000, would mean about 75,000,000 dollars of gross business. Broadcasting in that plan was a cost of selling sets and of publicizing the company, not a time-sales business. A later RCA committee, as reported by Eugene Lyons and quoted on the same page, found no way to support broadcasting without advertising. AT&T’s revealed toll price was the 2,600-dollar network hour. The FCC judged that AT&T “had been well on its way toward financial success” when it sold the business. So evening time was not later dumped below the builders’ own toll price. The 1929–32 equity collapse did not cut the card rate.

Idle capacity was unsold hours, not a surplus of dark towers. In 1931 Frank Arnold, NBC’s director of development, wrote that sustaining programs, paid for by the broadcaster, were more than two-thirds of national program hours on an 18-hour day, and that nearly 70 percent of one great system’s hours were still sustaining that spring. In 1926 a leading station’s operating cost was already near 30,000 dollars a month, against equipment of 10,000 to more than 1,000,000 dollars that went obsolete quickly. By 1938 the 310 stations unaffiliated with a national network had an aggregate net operating loss of 149,107 dollars. The networks were not idle: NBC’s time sales were 7,256,179 dollars in 1928, 11,353,120 in 1929, and 20,915,979 in 1932; CBS’s were 1,409,975, 4,720,074, and 10,218,800 dollars in those years.

ANSWER: The evening advertising market is mostly diffusion: it worked at card rates AT&T was already approaching profit on, and NBC’s time sales rose through the crash. The third-claim slice is the residue those cards did not clear: daytime hours at half the evening price, sustaining time that was still most of the national schedule in 1931, and the many stations that could not or would not pay AT&T for a chain. The set boom was capacity in the receiver market. It created the audience denominator. It did not, by itself, create a new industry. BENEFICIARY: Recorded syndication, the “chainless chain,” begun by Freeman Gosden and Charles Correll and copied as the transcription business
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: Sam ’n’ Henry on WGN, 12 January 1926. WGN refused to let them record it for other stations. They moved to WMAQ, owned by the Chicago Daily News, in December 1927, with syndication rights. Amos ’n’ Andy began 19 March 1928, recorded in advance on 12-inch 78-rpm discs and shipped to subscribing stations. Pepsodent took the live show to NBC Blue in August 1929. By the early 1930s the disc method was the basis of the syndication industry. Nightly audience of the network version exceeded 40 million in 1930–31.
CHEAP INPUT: Not the network hour. The input was airtime on stations outside a wired chain, reached by a disc instead of an AT&T line. The line was the input whose price did not fall: AT&T kept it and charged for it. HOW USED: Two performers recorded a 15-minute serial once and leased it to many stations, synchronizing the discs with the Chicago broadcast. Sponsors later used the same 16-inch electrical transcription to place a show on stations without buying the basic network or the long haul.

LAG: The disc network is 1928, about two years after NBC and a year before the crash. It is a response to station abundance and to wire charges, not to the RCA share collapse.
FORESEEN?: No. They tried to patent it and failed. Contemporaries who wanted national coverage were expected to buy the telephone chain.

BENEFICIARY: ASCAP and its composer and publisher members
TYPE: UNCLEAR (they are not downstream users of cheap transmission; they held the copyrights and charged the broadcasters)
DATES: ASCAP founded 1914 after Victor Herbert’s fight with Shanley’s restaurant. The radio fight starts in 1922, when Gene Buck heard his own songs on KDKA. Broadcasters formed the National Association of Broadcasters in 1923 to resist. Courts and Congress refused a compulsory nominal rate. In September 1932 the NAB agreed to 960,000 dollars a year plus 3 percent of advertising gross in 1933, 4 percent in 1934, and 5 percent in 1935. ASCAP collections about 2,000,000 dollars in 1932, estimated 4,500,000 dollars in 1935. AA writers then drew about 5,000 to 6,000 dollars a year from ASCAP, apart from sheet music and the theatre.
CHEAP INPUT: None that they bought. Music was the broadcasters’ raw material. ASCAP’s price rose as a share of advertising gross. The thing that became abundant was performances, which destroyed the parlor-piano sale and replaced it with a toll on the station.
HOW USED: They licensed the right to perform the repertoire and divided the take by an internal classification, not by a census of plays, because a play-by-play royalty account would have cost more than the collections.
MECHANISM: INSTITUTIONAL
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The copyrights, which kept durable value. Buck’s threat in 1932 was that if radio dropped ASCAP music the public might reopen the parlor piano and “maybe we can sell a little sheet music again.” The gains went to the copyright owners, not to listeners.
LAG: About a decade from the 1922 fight to the 1932 percentage contract. Collections were already real before the stock crash.
FORESEEN?: The publishers foresaw unpaid performance immediately, in 1922. They did not foresee that the sheet-music loss would be replaced by a percentage of radio advertising.

BENEFICIARY: Network stars and the talent market that formed around them, exemplified by Gosden and Correll
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Professional sponsorship replaces unpaid amateurs across 1923–26. Amos ’n’ Andy’s network peak is 1930–31, nightly audience above 40 million. The star system is fully priced by the 1930s. CHEAP INPUT: The listener-hour assembled by the set boom. Talent was paid out of the sponsor’s budget on top of the time charge. The stars did not need time to be priced below P*. They needed one microphone to reach a national audience that vaudeville and a theatre could not.

FORESEEN?: Sarnoff foresaw “music recitals, lectures,” not a nightly serial personality earning a corporate tax shelter. Lescarboura in 1926 already saw that chain audiences were large enough to pay “the world’s leading artists.”

BENEFICIARY: Archibald Crossley’s Cooperative Analysis of Broadcasting, the first regular national program-rating service
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Crossley Inc. founded 1918. The Association of National Advertisers endorsed a listener study; by 1929, 30 sponsors had endorsed it; fieldwork began in 1930, in the 33 cities where networks had outlets. In 1934 the service passed to a cooperative of advertisers and agencies. Broadcasters joined the support later. CAB closed in June 1946 after Hooper’s coincidental telephone method took the clients.
CHEAP INPUT: None directly. The service measured the audience that sponsors were buying. JWT’s February 1925 objection, that radio had no circulation currency comparable to a magazine audit, is the demand. That demand exists at the 1925–26 card rates. It does not require a crash.
HOW USED: Next-day telephone recall, later coincidental calls, produced a program “rating” so a sponsor who owned the whole show could see whether the audience was large. Early samples were urban telephone homes, a shrinking share of radio homes by the late 1930s.
MECHANISM: INFORMATION/COORDINATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The method and the subscriber list. It did not keep durable value: Hooper displaced it, and CAB’s cooperative board was slower than a private firm. The gain was mostly to advertisers, who could shift spending, and to listeners only indirectly.
LAG: Fieldwork in 1930 is four years after NBC and one year after the crash. The commercial need was stated in 1925.
FORESEEN?: Agencies knew they lacked a currency. Nobody in the 1922 toll experiments was selling a rating. Crossley coined the industry use of the word.

BENEFICIARY: The radio-repair trade
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Grows with the installed base through the 1930s. In 1939 the Census counted 10,732 repair establishments, 11,000 proprietors, 2,591 employees, and 21,700,000 dollars of receipts, about 2,025 dollars per shop.
CHEAP INPUT: Not airtime. The input is the stock of sets. Average retail set prices were high and rising in the 1920s, from 50 dollars in 1922 to 133 dollars in 1929 excluding tubes, then fell to 34 dollars in 1933. Repairs were economic because a set still cost on the order of a production worker’s weekly wages and tubes failed.
HOW USED: One-person shops fixed sets in place. This is a complement to receiver ownership, the same market the boom was built for, not a use of written-down transmission.
MECHANISM: COMPLEMENTARY-PRODUCT
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Local skill and the service call. Shops were tiny, so the scarcity did not capitalize into a durable firm. Gains went mostly to set owners as continued use of an expensive box.
LAG: A 1930s trade, several years after the set boom and the crash.
FORESEEN?: Sarnoff told dealers in 1922–23 that they would have to service what they sold. The scale of a 10,000-shop trade was not in the music-box memo.

TYPE: UNCLEAR (same-market survivors of the receiver glut, not a downstream industry)
DATES: Philco, a battery firm, shipped its first sets in 1928, 96,000 of them. Midgets appeared in California in 1929 from small assemblers. Emerson had been a surplus-set dealer since 1924 and became the largest specialist in small sets. Midgets were 60 percent of unit sales from 1 December 1932 to 1 May 1933. Retail home-set price, excluding tubes, fell from 133 dollars in 1929 (4,438,000 units) to 34 dollars in 1933 (3,806,000 units). Output then rose to 6,030,000 sets in 1935.
CHEAP INPUT: The factory price of a receiver, which fell after 1929 because of overcapacity, distress inventory, and a simpler cabinet. That is more sets for set buyers. It is the exclusion case in the brief.
HOW USED: They sold a cheap second or first set to households the console trade had priced out, through general stores and mail order rather than the specialist radio dealer.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Philco’s existing electrical distribution, and the mail-order houses’ catalogs. Brand leadership did not stick: of the four 1929 volume leaders, Atwater Kent, Grigsby-Grunow, and Crosley could not hold their place; RCA stayed, mainly on patents, tubes, and broadcasting. In 1940 the largest receiver firm had 14.4 percent of sales and the largest three 37.4 percent. Gains went largely to consumers as lower prices.
LAG: The price break is 1930–33, immediately after the set boom and the crash. Take-off of the small set is the early 1930s.
FORESEEN?: No. 1920s leaders competed on annual model changes, consoles, and door-to-door demonstration. The midget was treated as a depression novelty.

BENEFICIARY: National political broadcasting, from Coolidge’s networked addresses through the 1928 campaign; the fireside chats of 1933 are a later use of the same arrangement
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Coolidge on a 23-station AT&T hookup in autumn 1924; inaugural, 4 March 1925, 22 stations, audience estimated at 18,000,000. Message to Congress carried 4 December 1923. Arnold, writing in 1931, calls the 1928 presidential campaign the first real test of political broadcasting, with time offered to parties on the same terms. The fireside chats begin in 1933, after that system existed.
CHEAP INPUT: Sustaining time, which the president did not buy at the evening card. It was abundant because most national hours were still unsold in 1931, but a president would have been carried at the commercial P* as well. This is not a business whose model required the write-down.
HOW USED: Candidates and office-holders spoke to households without a newspaper or a hall as intermediary. The 1928 audience could hear both sides at home.
MECHANISM: INFORMATION/COORDINATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The office and the party organization. No firm captured a durable toll. The networks kept the privilege of carrying the president, which was worth more as prestige than as a time sale.
LAG: National political address is simultaneous with the first chains, 1923–25. The chats lag the crash by about four years and the first coast-to-coast political broadcast by about eight.
FORESEEN?: Yes. Sarnoff’s text says events of national importance “can be simultaneously announced and received.” RCA’s September 1926 announcement hoped every such event would be broadcast. Hoover feared advertising, not politics.

BENEFICIARY: The television networks of the 1940s and 1950s
TYPE: UNCLEAR (they fail the downstream test: they were NBC and CBS, the same operators)
DATES: Arnold in 1931 already called television nearly ready. RCA began experimental broadcasting in New York on 30 April 1939. Consumer television is a late-1940s business. P&G, which had incorporated a radio-serial subsidiary in 1940, produced the first network television soap, The First Hundred Years, in 1950. The Guiding Light, on radio from 1937, went to television in 1952 and was simulcast for a time.
CHEAP INPUT: Not a written-down radio tower. What transferred was affiliate relationships, advertiser budgets, agencies, serial writers, and studio organizations. Television needed new transmitters and, generally, new antennas. No fetched page shows the 1920s towers being reused as the television plant.
HOW USED: The same sponsors moved household brands from radio daytime to television daytime. That is the builder and his customers changing medium, not a third party buying stranded radio capacity.
MECHANISM: OTHER (reuse of contracts and program craft by the incumbents)
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The network franchise and the sponsor’s serial ownership. Both kept their value. RCA’s 1932 shareholders did not, as a class, get a distressed claim on television; RCA the company developed it and survived.
LAG: About twenty years from NBC (1926) to a mass television audience after the war, and about ten years from the 1939 experimental start.
FORESEEN?: Yes, as a technical sequel. Arnold’s 1931 chapter list includes television. The daytime-serial transplant was not what Sarnoff’s music-box plan described.

QUOTE: "RCA began experimental television broadcasting in New York on April 30, 1939, but World War II and technical problems prevented TV from becoming a consumer good until the late 1940s."

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: There was no receivership of the broadcasting plant and no sale of a national tower system at a few cents on the dollar. Four different transfers are easy to mix up. First, AT&T sold a going concern. WEAF and the broadcasting business, except WCAP, which was closed, went to RCA for 1,000,000 dollars under a contract of 1 July 1926 effective 1 November 1926. NBC paid it. AT&T agreed not to compete in broadcasting for seven years or repay 800,000 dollars, and agreed to furnish the wires. The FCC says AT&T was already on the way to making toll broadcasting pay. This was a strategic exit, not a distress sale, and the wires, the durable piece, stayed with AT&T. Second, the RCA share collapse was a write-down by the same equity holders. The price fell from 114.75 in September 1929 to about 2.50–2.63 in May 1932, roughly 98 percent, without a broadcast receivership. Third, control of RCA moved by antitrust, not by bankruptcy. The consent decree of 21 November 1932 ordered General Electric and Westinghouse to divest their RCA shares to their own stockholders, half of the common within three months and the rest within three years, and to take their officers off RCA’s board. Pending divestiture they could not freely vote the stock. That dispersed the parents’ control. It did not convey towers at a fraction of construction cost. Fourth, the only clearly distressed broadcast property in this episode is the early Columbia chain. United Independent Broadcasters’ first affiliate contract, September 1927, paid each of 16 stations 50 dollars an hour for 10 specified hours a week whether or not the hours were used, and the FCC says United lost so heavily that the contract was replaced in December 1927. In 1928 William S. Paley bought 41 percent and his family the rest of what became CBS. The encyclopedia entry does not state the dollar price. Later, and outside the 1929 crash, the FCC’s chain-broadcasting case led RCA to shed the Blue network. Finaeon says that sale, which it dates to the 1941 proceeding, was to Edward J. Noble for 8,000,000 dollars, with the name ABC adopted in 1946. Set makers did fail in the ordinary way: Kolster Radio went from 10 to 95 between 1927 and 1929 and below 1 in 1930, when it filed for bankruptcy. That was receiver capacity, not the network.

UNCERTAIN: Paley’s dollar outlay for CBS, and whether Noble’s 8,000,000 dollars was a small fraction of Blue’s cost, are not on the pages fetched. Finaeon compresses the Blue sale into 1941; the standard closing date is 1943, which these pages do not confirm.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Almost all of the economic mass was short-lived equipment and an organizational franchise, not earthworks or cable in the ground. Lescarboura’s 1926 survey put a leading station’s operating cost near 30,000 dollars a month, 360,000 dollars a year, a department-store station near 60,000 dollars a year, and a modest station upward of 25,000 dollars a year, against a plant of anywhere from 10,000 dollars to more than 1,000,000 dollars “at a rate of obsolescence which is positively appalling.” The FCC in 1941 said outright that broadcasting does not require large capital investment. At the end of 1938 the tangible broadcasting property of NBC and CBS together was 9,276,019 dollars, and their net operating income that year, 9,277,352 dollars, exceeded it. NBC’s tangible plant was 4,284,032 dollars; CBS’s was 4,991,988 dollars. Their entire broadcasting investment, tangible and intangible, was 13,411,102 dollars. Set production was a different order of size: 411,637,000 dollars of radio apparatus and tubes in 1929, and 842,500,000 dollars of retail equipment sales. Those factories and the annual receiver models did not survive as a stranded asset into the beneficiaries’ period. Consoles were obsolete by the midget years.

What did survive, and what the beneficiaries actually used, was: AT&T’s long-distance wires, which AT&T never sold; the license and the clear-channel assignments, an institutional right created by the Radio Act of 1927 and the Communications Act of 1934 rather than by the stock issue; affiliate contracts and advertiser habit; studio technique; and the installed audience, even though each set was short-lived. By the end of 1938, NBC and CBS affiliates held 50 of 52 clear-channel stations and about 85 percent of national nighttime wattage on the unlimited-time stations. That scarcity was regulatory, not a sunk tower. Early 1920s transmitters, tents-on-the-roof studios, and low-power sets were gone before Ma Perkins and the rating services mattered.

QUOTE: "The broadcasting industry does not require large capital investments. The NBC and CBS investment in tangible property devoted to broadcasting at the end of 1938 totaled $9,276,019."

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The displaced incumbents were in adjacent entertainment and in the set trade, not a single pre-radio advertiser. Sheet music and the parlor piano lost the household performance that had paid popular composers; ASCAP’s president used the reopening of the parlor piano as a threat, which only makes sense if radio had already closed it. Phonograph demand fell because the program was free. RCA bought Victor Talking Machine for the Camden factory and the dealer network, to sell radios, not because records were the growth business. Victor’s own stock had risen from 32 in July 1927 to 190 in April 1929 before that logic fully bit. Local amateur programs lost the audience to professional sponsored shows. Lescarboura in 1926 already described smaller stations’ amateurs being neglected once leading stations could hire talent. Vaudeville lost a share of the evening, and Surdam notes that movies and radio replaced it, but these pages do not quantify vaudeville closures. Newspapers threatened, in Printer’s Ink on 8 February 1923, to withdraw free publicity if broadcasters took advertising. They were not destroyed. Many became station licensees, and the medium they feared became a customer for program listings.

Inside radio, the losers were the specialist dealer and three of the four 1929 set leaders. From 1929 to the late 1930s, output of sets rose from 4,440,000 to 6,030,000 in 1935, while employment in the “household appliances, radio dealers” class fell 34.8 percent, to 71,971, dollar sales of those stores fell 53.4 percent, and the number of dealers fell 26.3 percent. Atwater Kent, Grigsby-Grunow, and Crosley could not survive the midget on the old scale. Crosley continued, reduced. Unaffiliated stations, as a class, were not gifted a cheap audience: in 1938 the 310 of them posted an aggregate operating loss of 149,107 dollars, while NBC and CBS and their own stations took about half the industry’s operating income. Baseball owners who had feared radio were only partly wrong. Set ownership was associated with lower club net income. Allowing the broadcast raised attendance about 12 percent of the mean and did not significantly raise net income.

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: Yes. Most of the 1920s overbuild was receiver capacity aimed at households, and the 1930s price break in that market, from 133 dollars a set in 1929 to 34 dollars in 1933, excluding tubes, predicts exactly what happened: exit of famous console makers, a shift from specialist dealers to general retailers, and no auto-style oligopoly. It does not predict soap operas or rating firms. There was also a cross-market input. The metered unit was the sponsored hour, in dollars per clock-hour, daytime at half the evening card. Evening network time did not fall below the price at which AT&T was already nearing commercial success, about 2,600 dollars an hour for 13 stations at the end of 1925, grossing about 750,000 dollars a year. National evening advertising is therefore diffusion. It would have been bought at that P* once the audience was large, and it was. Two downstream uses did depend on a price the evening toll plan did not clear. Daytime serials used hours the card itself marked at half, in a schedule that in 1931 was still more than two-thirds sustaining. Disc syndication used stations while avoiding AT&T’s line charge, which never collapsed because AT&T kept the wires. Those are the third claims. The fireside chat and the later television network do not pass the test. One used free sustaining time a president would have been given anyway. The other was the same companies moving their own advertisers.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: The counts are not on one denominator, and the usual “thousands of makers collapsed to a handful” story is too strong. In 1926, when NBC was launched, the New York Times figure cited by Scott and Walker is some 2,000 manufacturers selling through about 31,000 retailers and 1,000 wholesalers, to an audience of about 20,000,000 people owning 5,000,000 receivers. That 2,000 includes the small assemblers the census later dropped. The Census of Manufactures establishments reported in the Statistical Abstract, a class that by 1931 is “radio apparatus and phonographs,” were 217 in 1931, 156 in 1933, 196 in 1935, 162 in 1937, and 224 in 1939. So from the mid-1920s trade count to the mid-1930s census count the population of firms fell by roughly an order of magnitude, while the census count itself stayed in the 150–220 range across the Depression. In 1933 the largest nine set makers were said to have 74 percent of industry turnover. In 1940 the largest firm had 14.4 percent of sales and the largest three 37.4 percent. The 1947 Census four-firm ratio was 25.6 percent, and the top twenty had 54.4 percent. Maclaurin’s verdict, quoted by Domazlicky, is that margins had largely disappeared for many companies by the end of the 1920s. The shakeout removed the fringe and several famous 1929 names. It left a monopolistically competitive set industry, not a three-firm survivor group.

Part C

1. The hour got more expensive while the listener got cheaper. Contemporaries in 1926 already called New York time dear, at 600 dollars an evening hour, and a leading station cost about 30,000 dollars a month to run. The draft’s picture, that crashed RCA capital left towers whose use was suddenly bargained away, misses the rate card. Evening prices rose from the 1922 talk to the 1926 chain to the 1,200-dollar New York hour of the mid-1930s. The collapse that mattered to a sponsor was in the denominator, households, and in the daypart the card had already marked down by half before the stock broke.
SOURCE: https://earlyradiohistory.us/1926how.htm
QUOTE: "It costs $600.00 per hour to broadcast a sponsored program from one of the leading stations in that city, or $375.00 for half an hour"

2. The idle asset was unsold hours, and it was still idle after the networks were profitable. Arnold in 1931, from inside NBC, put sustaining programs at more than two-thirds of an 18-hour national day. Sponsors were not picking over a bankrupt plant. They were buying the minority of hours the broadcaster had managed to sell, while the station filled the rest at its own expense. That is why a 15-minute weekday serial, with an organ and a small cast, could be a business in 1933 when an evening spectacular looked, to a small advertiser, like gambling at 20 dollars a minute.
SOURCE: https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Business/Broadcast-Advertising-1931.pdf
QUOTE: "These sustaining programs at the present time comprise more than two-thirds of the program hours broadcast nationally, based on an output of eighteen program hours daily."

3. The one metered input that stayed dear was the wire, and AT&T never wrote it down. The 1,000,000-dollar sale conveyed WEAF, not the long lines. The transcription business exists because of that fact. Gosden and Correll’s discs were a way to be national without the chain charge Lescarboura measured as the gap between a station’s own 120-dollar hour and its 200-dollar chain hour. Accounts that narrate “the network” as the cheap residue have it backwards. The network hour was the expensive, rationed product. The cheap residue was everybody the network did not reach, plus the daytime hours the network could not clear.
SOURCE: https://earlyradiohistory.us/1941cb01.htm
QUOTE: "The Telephone Co. also agreed to make available its telephone lines to RCA for network purposes"

4. The manufacturing shakeout did not behave like the usual boom-and-bust consolidation, and the dealer was the unexpected loser. Prices of sets rose from 50 dollars in 1922 to 133 dollars in 1929, then fell to 34 dollars in 1933, while unit output in 1935 was higher than in 1929. Households gained a cheap set. The specialist retailer, whose model was the console, the home demonstration, and the instalment contract, lost 26.3 percent of shops and 53.4 percent of dollar sales. Philco and Emerson and the mail-order brands were not a new downstream industry. They were the firms that could live in the original market after the price fell. And even they did not concentrate it: the top three held 37.4 percent of sales in 1940.
SOURCE: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/48682/3/US%20radio%20marketing%20paper%20formtted%20March%202015.pdf
QUOTE: "employment in the ‘household appliances, radio dealers’ Census classification had fallen by 34.8 per cent, to 71,971."

5. Looking at the RCA share price was a bad way to see who held the scarcity. By 1938 the two networks’ operating income for one year exceeded their entire tangible plant of 9,276,019 dollars. The 98 percent equity loss was real for shareholders, and the 1932 decree then handed GE’s and Westinghouse’s RCA shares to those companies’ own stockholders. Neither event stranded the audience, the license, or the wire. Unaffiliated stations, the firms that looked most like buyers of surplus capacity, lost money in aggregate in 1938. The rent sat in the affiliate contract and the New York card, which is why a cigar maker’s son could buy a failing program chain in 1928 and why Noble, much later, would pay 8,000,000 dollars for Blue. Contemporaries watching Kolster go bankrupt, or RCA touch 2.50 dollars, reasonably thought “radio” had been liquidated. The liquidated piece was the claim on set profits. The piece that compounded was a small stock of wires, licenses, and habits.
