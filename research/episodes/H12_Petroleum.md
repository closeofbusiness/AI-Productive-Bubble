PART B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was shallow wells, iron stills, wooden tankage, and then wrought-iron pipe, all sized to kerosene. Physical scale: about 4,500 barrels of Pennsylvania crude in 1859; half a million barrels in 1860; over 2 million in 1861; about 3 million by 1862. Pithole in October 1865 produced nearly two-thirds of North American petroleum, on the order of 6,000 barrels a day, against a five-mile pipeline of about 2,500 barrels a day. Cleveland in 1865 had about 30 refineries, about $1.5 million of capital, and about 2,000 barrels a day of capacity; by 1869 the Board of Trade put refining capital at not less than $4 million. U.S. refining capacity in 1872 was 46,571 barrels a day. By 1882 Standard controlled about 3,000 miles of pipeline. In 1900 U.S. output was 63.6 million barrels at $1.19 a barrel, worth $75.7 million, 0.4 percent of estimated GNP of $18,700 million. Pennsylvania and New York together in 1890 produced five times their 1870 output, then the Appalachian peak passed.
Metered inputs: (1) crude petroleum, dollars per 42-gallon barrel, Oil Creek points before 1875 and pipeline certificates after; (2) refined kerosene, cents per gallon, New York, in barrels, tax excluded; (3) short-haul transport, dollars per barrel over a few miles.
P*, the return the plans required: seep oil before Drake sold at 75 cents to $2 a gallon (Derrick; Hamilton’s top of that range is about $80 a barrel at 40 gallons). Samuel Kier sold distilled “carbon oil” at $1.50 a gallon. Drake’s first product brought 50 cents a gallon, and crude is quoted at $20 a barrel from August through December 1859. On 19 December 1872 the Producers’ Association and the Refiners’ Association, John D. Rockefeller signing for the refiners, set crude conditionally at $5 per 42-gallon barrel at common points when New York refined sold at 26 cents a gallon. The 1872 South Improvement contracts set an open rail rate of $2.56 a barrel on crude to New York, with a $1.06 rebate to members. Rockefeller’s own export realization fell from 58¾ cents a gallon in 1865, margin 43 cents, to 26⅜ cents in 1870, margin 17⅛ cents.
Price path, crude, yearly average, dollars per barrel: 1859 $20; 1860 $9.60; 1861 $0.525 (December low $0.105); 1862 $1.05; 1864 $8.15 (July high $14); 1866 $3.75 (December low $1.35); 1870 $3.90; 1872 $3.75; 1874 $1.15; 1879 about $0.86; 1892 about $0.56; 1900 about $1.35 on the Derrick average. Hamilton’s 1900 U.S. average is $1.19. The 1872 cartel figure of $5 was already above the 1872 market average of $3.75, and the 1874 average of $1.15 was far below it.
Idle capacity: around 1871, refining capacity was three barrels for each barrel produced, so nameplate capacity was about three times throughput. Tarbell notes that even prosperous works always had some stills down, so the idle share is not a clean two-thirds. UNCERTAIN: the operating-day fraction. Gathering lines were full. At Pithole the constraint was teamsters and tankage. Wells there went dry. They did not sit as spare capacity.

QUESTION: B2
ANSWER: Ranked by how large a use they made of this boom’s cheap crude and its cuts. The first is the builders’ own market, enlarged. The next six are downstream of a cut whose price lay below the lamp-oil plan. The last four are the leads that belong to later oil, or to imported asphalt. Kerosene lighting, factory lubricants, and railroad oils dominate the 19th-century effect. Paint solvents, gas machines, and Vaseline are the non-obvious three.

BENEFICIARY: Household, commercial, and export kerosene lighting, including the lamp trade that lengthened working and reading hours
TYPE: UNCLEAR (same customer the wells were drilled for: lamp users. The price collapse enlarged that market down the demand curve. More kerosene for lamp buyers predicts consolidation among refiners, which is what happened, rather than a new industry.)
DATES: Kier’s Pittsburgh still and $1.50-a-gallon carbon oil in the 1850s; Drake well 27 August 1859; mass take-off with the 1860–61 flood. Export was already large by 1871, when Tarbell ranks oil fourth among U.S. exports twelve years after discovery. Standard alone made 68,518,424 of kerosene in 1873 beside 12,424,006 gallons of naphtha, and called 77 percent of that production kerosene.
CHEAP INPUT: Refined kerosene, cents per gallon, New York, in barrels. Export realization 58¾ cents a gallon in 1865 versus 26⅜ cents in 1870. Yearly average 44¾ cents in 1863, 26⅜ cents in 1870, 13 cents in 1874, 7⅜ cents in 1890, 5.24 cents in 1893. Pre-boom benchmark: Kier at $1.50 a gallon, and seep oil at 75 cents to $2 a gallon. One gallon of kerosene was credited with about 140 hours of illumination.
HOW USED: Households, shops, and foreign buyers burned it in lamps. Philadelphia’s pipeline history ties that brighter, cheaper light to longer hours of work. The oil was also canned and exported. The scarce thing on the producer side became distribution and brand, held by Standard, while the gallon price fell by a factor of roughly eight from the mid-1860s to the early 1890s.
MECHANISM: COMPLEMENTARY-PRODUCT
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: A safe lamp, and then Standard’s bulk distribution and export brands. The gallon price fell from the high tens of cents to about 5–8 cents, so a large share of the gain went to consumers. Standard kept the refining and marketing rent.
LAG: About 0–2 years from the 1859 wells to the first flood; the 1861 crash is the moment the input became cheap.
FORESEEN?: Yes. Benjamin Silliman Jr.’s report told the Pennsylvania Rock Oil Company it held a raw material for valuable products and specified lamp kerosene. Kier was already selling lamp oil.

BENEFICIARY: Cotton and woolen mills, and other factories, oiling spindles, wool, and machinery with petroleum neutral and paraffin oils
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: For several years after 1859 only kerosene was treated as valuable. By 1873 the Downer works at Boston and Corry sold 600,000 gallons of neutral lubricating oil, and the 1879 Cyclopaedia calls the substitution immense. Take-off is the early 1870s.
CHEAP INPUT: Heavy distillate and still residuum, sold when light ends and bottoms were worth little. U.S. landed sperm oil had already risen from 63 cents a gallon in 1843 to $1.21 in 1858, on falling supply.

BENEFICIARY: Railroads and ocean steamers, using mineral sperm in lamps and petroleum oils on journals and axles
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: Mineral sperm is credited to Joshua Merrill of the Downer company, in commercial use by 1873 (Downer sold 250,000 gallons of mineral sperm that year). Journal oils are described as established by 1879.
CHEAP INPUT: A high-boiling petroleum cut (mineral sperm, specific gravity about 0.847, boiling near 425°F) and heavier lubricating oils. Mineral sperm would not take fire below 300°F. The benchmark it beat on cost was whale and sperm oil at well over a dollar a gallon in the late 1850s, against kerosene already in the mid-20s of cents a gallon by 1870 and heavier cuts cheaper still.
HOW USED: Railroads and steamships needed an illuminant that was hard to ignite in a boiler room or car, and a journal oil cheaper than sperm. The Cyclopaedia names those carriers as the users of mineral sperm. Ordinary dark paraffin oils went onto heavy bearings.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Flash-point specification and the blending recipe. Carriers did not own the oil. As neutral oils became ordinary, the price cut went to the railroads and shipowners. A few compounders kept a premium for treated oils.
LAG: About 10–15 years from 1861; about 8 years from the mid-1860s refinery build-out to the 1873 Downer figures.
FORESEEN?: The safe lamp oil for ships and trains was a refiner’s invention of the 1860s–70s. It is not in the Silliman use-list as the ACS summarizes it. Railroad men did not plan the Pennsylvania boom in order to lubricate axles.

BENEFICIARY: Paint, varnish, and resin-solvent users of naphtha
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: In use by the 1870s and described in 1879 as a live but limited trade. Non-obvious relative to the lamp-oil story.
CHEAP INPUT: A, B, and C naphtha (specific gravities about 0.742, 0.724, and 0.706). The Cyclopaedia says vast quantities were burned under stills, thrown away, or “sold at the purchaser’s price,” and that as little as 5 percent of naphtha left in kerosene made the lamp oil explosive. This cut was priced below the kerosene plan, often at a disposal price.
HOW USED: Painters and varnish makers mixed paints, dissolved resins, and cut varnishes with it, in place of dearer turpentine. The buyers were manufacturers and painters, not household lamp customers.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Paint formulas and the painter’s customer book. Naphtha itself was not scarce. The Cyclopaedia still calls the commercial value small, so the rent did not stay with a naphtha brand. It went to whoever already sold paint.
LAG: About 10–15 years from the 1861 glut to a recognized solvent trade in the 1870s.
FORESEEN?: No in the 1855 lighting prospectus. Yes by 1879, and even then writers called the uses limited and the fluid nearly worthless. The non-obvious part is that a fraction refiners tried to dump became a solvent input.

BENEFICIARY: Separate paraffin and lubricating-oil works, and the candle trade they fed, buying still residuum from kerosene refineries
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: Separate residuum plants are described as already erected by 1879. Downer’s 1873 output included 500,000 pounds of paraffin wax. Silliman had named paraffin before the well.
CHEAP INPUT: Still residuum, the black bottoms after kerosene was taken off. The Cyclopaedia says naphthas and residuum near the wells were burned as fuel or sold to gasoline and paraffin manufacturers, and that the heavy products were “worth no more than cost.”
HOW USED: Specialists pressed wax from chilled paraffin oil and sold white wax, and sold heavy oil for coarse lubrication. Candle makers and wax users were a different set from kerosene-lamp buyers, even though both products light a room.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: Filter presses, sweating know-how, and a white-wax grade. Once many refineries recovered wax, the candle maker’s input got cheaper and the gain moved toward candle buyers. The process skill was copyable.
LAG: About 10–15 years from 1859–61 to the 1873–79 trade.
FORESEEN?: Paraffin itself, yes. Silliman said high-temperature distillation would yield it. A separate industry living on bottoms priced at cost was a response to the margin squeeze, not the original plan.

BENEFICIARY: Automatic gas machines for buildings off the coal-gas mains, burning gasolene
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: A recognized manufacture by 1879, when the Cyclopaedia says demand for gas machines had already caused plants that split gasolene out of heavier naphtha. Non-obvious: this is the pre-automobile use of the light fraction.
CHEAP INPUT: Gasolene, specific gravity about 0.665, boiling near 120°F, the lightest cut made in bulk. It was too volatile for lamps. Contemporary descriptions say gasoline was flared or dumped. It sold below any kerosene P* (the 1872 refiners’ benchmark was 26 cents a gallon for refined lamp oil, and naphtha was often unsaleable).
HOW USED: A carburetor on the premises turned gasolene into illuminating gas where there was no town-gas pipe. The customer was a building owner, not a kerosene jobber’s household trade and not, yet, a motorist.
MECHANISM: NEW-LOCATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The gas-machine apparatus. The fuel was the abundant cut. Machine makers could hold a design advantage. The fuel maker could not, because the cut was in surplus.
LAG: About 15–20 years from the 1861 crash to the 1879 description of a specialized gasolene manufacture.
FORESEEN?: Not by the lamp-oil prospectuses. By 1879 it was ordinary enough to be written up, and still described as a minor outlet next to kerosene.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: The losses were taken in layers, by the people who had paid boom prices, and then by sale into Standard. There was no nationalisation of the 1860s plant.
Producers and townsite speculators took the 1861–62 and 1866 write-downs themselves. Pithole’s Holmden farm, sold by Duncan and Prather for $2 million in September 1865, was bought by the Venango County commissioners in 1878 for $4.37. That is town real estate after the sand was drained, not a pipeline receivership. Thousands of acres, engines, and machinery at Pithole were sold for debts and taxes by the late 1860s. Buildings were carted off or sold as firewood.
Refineries changed hands by private purchase under threat, not by a court receiver. In 1872 Standard of Ohio, capital raised from $1,000,000 to $2,500,000 on 1 January, bought at least 21 of about 26 Cleveland refineries in about three months. Capacity in Cleveland under Standard went from about 1,500 barrels a day to about 10,000, over one-fifth of U.S. capacity of 46,571 barrels a day. PBS’s account says 22 of 26 in six weeks, February–March 1872. Sellers were paid in Standard stock or cash at Standard’s appraisal. Alexander, Scofield and Company put their plant at $150,000 and sold for about $65,000, “about forty or forty-five per cent” of that valuation, after spending over $50,000 on the works in the past year. Hanna, Baslington had paid $75,000 and were allowed $45,000. No fetched page gives one fraction for all 22 purchases. Poor plants were dismantled. Better ones were kept and upgraded.
The South Improvement Company did not take title. Its charter was repealed by Pennsylvania in April 1872. PBS says it never conducted a transaction. Tarbell records that a Lake Shore clerk posted the new rates on 26 February 1872 anyway. The open rate to New York was $2.56 a barrel, with a $1.06 rebate and a matching drawback on outsiders’ barrels. The scheme’s aim was to hold refined prices up and stop crude exports so that foreigners would buy American refined.
Pipelines: Tidewater built a competing 109-mile line in 1879. Standard built its own trunk lines (Cleveland line 1880, seaboard lines 1881–83) and controlled about 3,000 miles by 1882. A Pennsylvania statute in 1883 forbade a Standard–Tidewater merger. The companies pooled, and Standard kept the dominant share. The Hepburn Act in 1906 made interstate oil pipelines common carriers. The 1911 decree broke Standard into 34 companies. That is later regulated access and antitrust division, not the mechanism of the 1860s–1872 write-down.
Kerosene prices after the Cleveland purchases, New York, cents per gallon, yearly average: 1871, 24¼; 1872, 23⅝; 1873, 18¼; 1874, 13. Crude averages in those years: 1872, $3.75 a barrel; 1874, $1.15. The merger was designed to stop a price fall. The fall continued with the crude glut. QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Most of the 1859–1866 boom was short-lived equipment and a depleting sand. Wooden derricks, wooden tanks, glued barrels, and Pithole’s wooden town did not last. The 2 August 1866 fire burned 27 wells and 13,000 barrels of oil. By 1870 a handful of people remained. The $2 million farm of 1865 was a $4.37 county purchase in 1878.
Wrought-iron pipe lasted longer than wood and was the beginning of a durable corridor. The five-mile Van Syckel line of 1865 was a local gathering line, fully used, then left behind when Pithole died. The pipes that still mattered when kerosene was a mass export were built later for Bradford and the seaboard: Tidewater in 1879, Standard’s trunk lines in 1880–83, about 3,000 miles under Standard by 1882. Rights-of-way and those later lines are the long-lived substrate. Refinery sites at Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, and New York also persisted, but the stills were rebuilt. Standard scrapped the bad 1872 plants.
The oil in the rock did not survive into the motor age. Pennsylvania production fell 14 million barrels from 1891 to 1894 and never regained the 1891 peak even with later secondary recovery. What did survive into the beneficiaries’ growth, meaning the 1870s–1890s kerosene and byproduct trades, was refining know-how, Standard’s organization, export channels, and the post-1878 pipeline grid. What survived into 1908 was that organization and an eastern refining geography, fed by new crude. The 1860s wells were not the tankage of the Model T.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The cheap barrel displaced other illuminants and lubricants, and it displaced the teamsters who had moved the oil.
Sperm oil was already failing before Drake. U.S. imports were 5.3 million gallons in 1843 and 2.6 million gallons in 1858, while the landed price rose from 63 cents a gallon to $1.21. North Pacific whaling vessels fell from 278 in 1852 to 32 in 1862, recovered only to 95 in 1866, and were 8 in 1876. Alexander Starbuck, in the 1877 federal fishery report, said Pennsylvania oil then outcompeted whale oil once its dangers were reduced. That is the U.S. lighting and lubricating fishery. Twentieth-century whaling was not ended by kerosene. Factory ships continued, and whale oil was hydrogenated in Germany in 1907 for margarine.
Camphene and alcohol lamp fuels lost on price and on tax. The federal alcohol tax rose from 20 cents a gallon in 1862 to $2 a gallon by 1865, against a 10-cent-a-gallon tax on petroleum illuminants. Hamilton treats that gap, at about 20 gallons of illuminant per barrel of crude, as about $2 a barrel of advantage to petroleum. Southern turpentine was also cut off by the war.
Coal-oil and shale-oil kerosene, the Gesner and Young trade, lasted on the order of ten years. The 1879 Cyclopaedia says that by about four years after petroleum refining spread, coal had been dropped even in the last plants. Downer’s Boston works, built for coal, boghead, and albertite, ran on nothing but petroleum after 1865.
Teamsters lost the short haul. They had charged about $4 a barrel, more than the rail rate onward to New York, with as many as 6,000 wagons a day on the bad roads, and more than 1,500 left Pithole by the end of 1865 after lines carried oil at about $1 a barrel. They tore up pipe until guards were posted.
Independent Cleveland refiners lost their plants at roughly 40 to 45 cents on their own valuation, as in B3. Small producers lost leases whenever the barrel went to 10 cents or, in 1866, to $1.35.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: The Cleveland purchases of February–March 1872 were a second-claim transfer. Standard, using the threat of South Improvement rebates and drawbacks, bought at least 21 of about 26 Cleveland refineries. Payment was Standard stock or cash, at Standard’s appraisal. The one sworn ratio fetched in full is Alexander, Scofield: about $65,000 for a works they valued at $150,000, 40 to 45 percent of that valuation, and they had spent over $50,000 in the previous year. Hanna, Baslington received $45,000 against a $75,000 cost. There is no fetched all-in ratio of purchase price to historical cost for the whole group. Standard’s capital step-up from $1 million to $2.5 million on 1 January 1872 is the financing vehicle, not a published purchase total.
South Improvement itself bought nothing that survived. It was a rebate-and-drawback contract with the Pennsylvania, the Erie, and the Central, organized in January 1872, exposed in February, charter repealed in April 1872. Rockefeller’s contemporaries were told the point was to stop competition and keep refined prices up, including a hoped-for 50 percent rise on export oil. It was not a plan to cheapen kerosene.
Observed New York kerosene after the purchases still fell, from 24.25 cents a gallon in 1871 to 13 cents in 1874, while crude fell from a 1872 average of $3.75 a barrel to a 1874 average of $1.15. The second-claim transfer concentrated refining. The price path of the gallon follows the crude glut. Calling the massacre the cause of cheaper kerosene overstates what the price series shows.

PART C

1. The boom’s own residue was more kerosene for lamp buyers. Silliman’s report and Kier’s still already defined the product. Surplus stills, at three barrels of capacity per barrel produced, are what Standard bought. That is the consolidation path. It is not a prediction that idle 1860s pipe would call the automobile into being. Appalachian output peaked in 1891, fell 14 million barrels by 1894, and never regained that peak.
SOURCE: https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jhamilto/oil_history.pdf
QUOTE: "Annual production from Pennsylvania fell by 14 million barrels between 1891 and 1894"

2. The light fraction was a waste problem, and its first real customers were not cars. Naphtha was burned under stills or sold at the buyer’s price. Gasolene fed automatic gas machines. Rhigolene, the most volatile cut, was made for Dr. H. J. Bigelow of Boston and used to drop local temperature to −19°F for surgical anesthesia. Otto’s 1876 engine, the one in the draft, was bolted to a workshop floor and fed from a town-gas pipe. Benz’s gasoline car is 1885–86, a prototype. The cheap-gasoline mechanism and the “pipes waiting for motors” mechanism are different, and only the first fits the 1870s.
SOURCE: https://www.howengineswork.com/timelines/engines/otto
QUOTE: "It made about three horsepower at 180 rpm, bolted to a workshop floor and fed from the town-gas pipe in the wall."

3. Asphalt avenues of the 1870s were Trinidad lake asphalt. Grant’s Pennsylvania Avenue, about 54,000 square yards by 1876, was imported bitumen. Petroleum asphalt is a bottom-of-the-still product that the American Oil & Gas Historical Society dates as a market force from about 1902, with Texas plants and Sun Oil, dominant within a decade, beside the automobile. Pennsylvania’s first-boom crude was the world’s lamp and lubricant crude. The road binder that paved the motor age is a later residue.
SOURCE: https://aoghs.org/products/asphalt-paves-the-way/
QUOTE: "Pennsylvania Avenue was first paved in 1876-77 with bitumen imported from Trinidad."

4. Kerosene’s victory over camphene had an institutional push. The alcohol tax went from 20 cents a gallon in 1862 to $2 a gallon by 1865, against 10 cents a gallon on petroleum illuminants. At a yield of about 20 gallons of illuminant per barrel, each 10-cent tax gap was worth about $2 a barrel to petroleum. The 10-cent crude crash of 1861 is real. It is not the only reason the older lamp fluids died. Coal oil, separately, was gone within about ten years because petroleum undercut it at the still.
SOURCE: https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jhamilto/oil_history.pdf
QUOTE: "the introduction of a tax on alcohol, which rose from 20¢/gallon in 1862 to $2/gallon by 1865"

5. Two further misses. U.S. sperm-oil lighting was already shrinking before 1859, and petroleum did not stop modern whaling. Imports halved from 5.3 million gallons in 1843 to 2.6 million in 1858 while the price doubled to $1.21 a gallon. Factory-ship whaling continued, and in 1907 whale oil was hydrogenated in Germany into margarine. And the 1860s system was not a pool of idle durable capital. Van Syckel’s line ran at about 2,000 barrels a day immediately. At Pithole the scarce piece was tankage and a $4 teamster haul. The surface speculation died: a $2 million farm in September 1865, $4.37 to the county in 1878.
