BENEFICIARY: Chicago Board of Trade and the grain futures trade
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: CBOT founded 3 April 1848 as a cash grain market; forward “to-arrive” contracts at once. Standardised to-arrive terms in 1858. An Illinois charter of 18 February 1859 standardised grades and made inspectors’ decisions binding. Formal margin and delivery rules on 13 October 1865. The CFTC’s older annual reports treated 1859 as the start of futures in CBOT wheat, corn, and oats; later CBOT documents treated regular publication of futures prices in 1877 as the start of “true” futures.
CHEAP INPUT: The inland telegram carrying a price. Buffalo wheat and corn prices lagged New York by four days in 1846 and were set simultaneously once the two markets were linked in 1848. A 10-word New York–Chicago message was still $1.55 in 1850 and $1.00 in 1870, so the futures rules of 1865 were written while the message was dear. The later fall to $0.40 by 1890 widened the net of who could afford continuous quotes. It was not a precondition for the exchange.
HOW USED: Graded, fungible grain could be sold by description. The wire carried the description and the price to eastern buyers, and the to-arrive contract let a Chicago seller commit grain still in the country. Yates’s point, following DuBoff, is that nonspecific graded commodities are exactly the goods a telegraph market can coordinate. This is more of the market the telegraph was built to serve.
MECHANISM: INFORMATION/COORDINATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The grade standards, the charter, and the rulebook. Those stayed with the exchange and kept their value. Tighter prices passed part of the gain to merchants and millers. Nonnenmacher also notes the offset: once everyone has the price, the race to be first need not raise wealth, and it raises the payoff to corners.
LAG: Price integration in 1848 is simultaneous with the founding of the CBOT, about two years after the first commercial US line. Formal futures rules in 1865 are about 17 years after 1848.
FORESEEN?: The price use was immediate and expected by merchants. A national futures market with margins was not in the early telegraph prospectuses; it was built by the exchange over the next seventeen years.

BENEFICIARY: New York Stock Exchange and the brokerage houses that traded on its prices
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: Yates places the NYSE’s emergence as the national stock market between 1850 and 1880. By 1910, 90 percent of bond trades and two-thirds of stock trades were on the NYSE. The quotation ticker is a separate, later layer: Gold and Stock Telegraph Company, incorporated August 1867 to exploit Edward Calahan’s ticker; Western Union took control in May 1871.
CHEAP INPUT: The same inland telegram, and then a dedicated ticker circuit. Chicago–New York was $2.05 for the first 10 words in 1869 on Yates’s reading, and $0.50 in the 1870s and 1880s. Stocks are a one-line description, so a short expensive message still cleared.

FORESEEN?: Trading on telegraphed prices was an early and obvious use. Calahan, a Western Union operator on the floor, designed the ticker for that use; it was foreseen inside the industry, not in the original Morse scheme.

BENEFICIARY: British provincial newspapers under the Post Office press rate
TYPE: THIRD CLAIM
DATES: The Press Association was formed in November 1865 by John Edward Taylor of the Manchester Guardian, while the wires were still private, because the companies would not release newspapers from news-gathering contracts. The Post Office took the inland telegraphs in February 1870. Perry records provincial papers receiving news by telegraph rising from 144 to 365 under the Post Office.
CHEAP INPUT: A press telegram under section 16 of the Telegraph Act 1868: not above 1 shilling per 100 words between 6 p.m. and 9 a.m., and not above 1 shilling per 75 words between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m., plus 2 pence per extra address, against an ordinary ceiling of 1 shilling per 20 words. Per word at night, the press ceiling is one-fifth of the ordinary ceiling. Perry records the subsidised press traffic producing a loss to the Post Office of £375,000 in 1900. The ordinary public average fell from 1s. 7d. under the companies to 1s. 1d. under the Post Office, then, after the 1885 cut, from 1s. 1d. to 8d. The press concession is the price that sits below what the purchase had to earn.
HOW USED: A provincial paper took one telegraphed report and printed it for local readers, a customer who never bought a telegram. The agency that pooled the copy (the Press Association) already existed at private rates. The statutory rate is what made the copy cheap enough to reach the larger set of papers, and the Post Office booked a loss on it.
MECHANISM: INPUT-COST
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: The newspaper title and the local readership. Those stayed with the publishers. Part of the gain was a transfer from taxpayers, because the rate did not cover the Post Office’s cost.
LAG: The agency is 1865, five years before the takeover and before the cheap rate. The jump from 144 to 365 papers is inside the first years of Post Office operation, so the lag from the subsidised rate to the wider provincial service is about 0 to 2 years.
FORESEEN?: Yes. The press lobbied for nationalisation, the Economist is quoted by Perry as saying no interest was so disliked by the press as the telegraph companies, and Parliament wrote the press tariff into the 1868 Act before the transfer.

BENEFICIARY: Coastal shipping under FitzRoy’s storm warnings
TYPE: DIFFUSION BENEFICIARY
DATES: The Meteorological Office had been collecting coastal observations since 1854. Telegraphed daily reports began 1 September 1860. The first storm warning was issued 5 February 1861. After FitzRoy’s death in 1865 the warnings were stopped, then restored in 1867 after losses rose. The service is the ancestor of the Shipping Forecast.
CHEAP INPUT: A telegraphed weather observation into London, and a warning back out to the coast. This was a government circuit on the inland telegraph, not a purchase of public telegrams at the company tariff. The Royal Charter gale of 25–26 October 1859, the disaster that produced the service, killed about 800 people and took 133 ships, with 90 more badly damaged.
HOW USED: Observations from around the coast were gathered by wire, a gale track was drawn, and cones and drums were hoisted so ships in harbour and along the coast could stay in. The industry that gained was coastal and short-sea shipping, including the insurers and owners who had borne the Royal Charter class of loss. It is a public service, not a private firm.
MECHANISM: INFORMATION/COORDINATION
SCARCE COMPLEMENT: FitzRoy’s method and the coastal display stations. The warnings were a public good. Shipowners kept the value in vessels not lost. There is no private franchise in the sources.
LAG: About 14 years from the first British commercial telegraph of the mid-1840s to the 1860 reports, and one season from the start of telegraphed observations to the first warning.
FORESEEN?: FitzRoy proposed it and the government allowed a trial. The scientific establishment doubted that a forecast was possible, and that doubt was strong enough to suspend the warnings in 1865. Mariners, including the RNLI’s community, treated the warnings as a success.

Feeder lines were absorbed by main lines by 1852; Bain lines merged into Morse lines after the patent case; the Six Nations pooled from 1857; in 1866 Western Union exchanged stock for the American Telegraph Company and the United States Telegraph Company. A later wave repeated the pattern: Jay Gould’s Atlantic and Pacific, formed in 1874, was merged into Western Union in 1881, and that year Western Union also bought the American Union (over 12,000 miles of line) and the Atlantic and Pacific (8,706 miles). No page reached gives the 1866 exchange ratio or the price as a fraction of historical cost. Britain, 1870: nationalisation at a premium. Section 8 of the Telegraph Act 1868 sets the price for the Electric, the Magnetic, and the United Kingdom companies at twenty years’ purchase of net profits for the year ending 30 June 1868, with extra sums for the United Kingdom company. Perry’s table of Scudamore’s estimates rises from £2.5 million in July 1866 to £3 million in February 1868 and £6.715 million in July 1869. Parliament then provided £8 million for purchase and extensions, and Scudamore spent a further £812,000 without authority. The Atlantic Telegraph Company and the Anglo-American were excluded by section 7. Railways kept the wires and poles needed to work their trains and were paid for wayleaves, lost public-message receipts, and a perpetual right of way. Atlantic cables: the 1858 plant was a total loss to its shareholders, about £500,000 on Cookson’s three early attempts, and it was not sold on as working capacity. The 1865 cable was recovered by the same promoting group and completed in September 1866. Anglo-American, capitalised at £600,000 of new money, ran a working agreement with the Atlantic company until it took that company over in 1873. Cookson’s judgment is that a working intercontinental cable had become exceptionally profitable. The second claim on the successful cables is the original shareholders, who were paid dividends, not a distressed buyer.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Most of what survived was the route and the right-of-way, not the original conductor. Land side: poles, railway wayleaves, and station instruments. The 1868 Act gave the railways their posts, wires, and instruments for train working, free, and gave the Post Office a perpetual right of way over the railways for the public wires. Wire and instruments were renewed in the ordinary course. Western Union’s wire rose from 97,594 miles in 1868 to 874,420 miles in 1898, which is new construction and absorption, not the 1851 plant sitting in place. Ocean side: the conductor was copper, gutta-percha, and iron armour, and it failed. The 1858 cable was dead within weeks. Glover records the 1866 cable not working by 1872, a major renewal in 1880, the 1865 cable ceasing in 1877, and the renewed 1866/1880 cable lasting until 1949 and then abandoned. The 1873, 1874, 1894, and 1910 Anglo-American cables were still operational when Western Union ended its lease in 1963. Users in the 1880s and after were on replacement cables and on a land network several times the mileage of 1866. The durable residue was landing rights, the charted route, the company, and the railway wayleave. It was not a sunk earthwork on the scale of a railroad grade.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The displaced parties were other sellers of the same services, and one old way of moving meat. Independent US telegraph companies lost their separate existence in the mergers of 1852–66 and again in 1881; their shareholders took stock in the survivor rather than being wiped out. In Britain the Electric, the Magnetic, the United Kingdom company, and the smaller firms ceased to operate in 1870, and their shareholders were paid twenty years’ profits. Regional US stock exchanges lost volume to New York: by 1910 the NYSE had 90 percent of bond trades and two-thirds of stock trades. Shippers of live cattle lost to dressed beef sent east at 50 percent of the live-animal freight. Coastal ships that sailed into unwarned gales were the loss the 1861 warnings were built to stop; the Royal Charter gale had taken about 800 lives and 133 ships. The private news contracts that provincial papers wanted out of were broken by nationalisation and replaced by the Press Association. Mail and the steamer still carried letters, newspapers, and long commercial circulars. Müller’s conclusion is that the ocean telegram never became a social medium: it stayed beyond the means of 99 percent of the population, and the letter remained the ordinary long-distance message.

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: Inland United States, company-wide average toll per message: $1.09 in 1867 (Nonnenmacher) and 104.7 cents in the year ending 30 June 1868, 38.9 cents in 1878, 31.2 cents in 1888, 30.1 cents in 1898 (Scientific American Reference Book), and 30 cents in 1900 (Nonnenmacher). On the New York–Buffalo route, entry in the early 1850s cut rates by more than half; the page does not give the cent price. Inland Britain: a uniform 1 shilling for 20 words had applied between certain large cities and was withdrawn in 1865 for a higher schedule. The 1868 Act capped the ordinary inland telegram at 1 shilling for the first 20 words, addresses not counted, and 3 pence for each further 5 words. Realised averages: 1s. 7d. under the companies, 1s. 1d. under the Post Office. The Telegraph Act 1885, 14 August 1885, capped the charge at 6 pence for the first 12 words including the names and addresses, and a halfpenny for each extra word. When that cut took effect, the average price fell from 1s. 1d. to 8d. Press ceiling from 1868: 1 shilling per 100 words at night and 1 shilling per 75 words by day. Atlantic, London–New York, Müller: £20 minimum for 20 words in summer 1866 (£1 per word); £10 for 10 words by the end of 1866; £5 for 10 words in 1867; £2 for 10 words from 1869; 4 shillings per word in 1872; 1 shilling per word in 1888, held until just after the First World War. Glover adds 1s 8d per word in 1884 and a Commercial Cable price war that touched 6d before returning to 1s. A London–Austin message in 1867 cost £6.67 at the ordinary 10-word cable rate of £5, because the landline and taxes sat on top. These ocean rates are the cable toll only as far as New York or Boston.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: The Post Office did not buy the companies at a discount to capital. For the three principal firms the statute fixes twenty years’ purchase of net profits for the year to 30 June 1868. Scudamore’s successive estimates of purchase price plus initial extensions were £2.5 million (July 1866), £3 million (February 1868), and £6.715 million (July 1869). Grants of £8 million for purchase and extensions were then exceeded by a further £812,000 spent without authority. Perry records 26 years before 1914 in which telegraph revenue did not cover expenditure, let alone interest on the purchase stock. The ordinary ceiling at the transfer was 1 shilling per 20 words, addresses free. Realised average cost fell from 1s. 7d. under the companies to 1s. 1d. under the Post Office. The sixpenny telegram is the 1885 statute, not the 1870 tariff: 6 pence for 12 words including the address. The average then fell from 1s. 1d. to 8d. The press rate, in the 1868 Act, was 1 shilling per 100 words at night and 1 shilling per 75 words by day. Provincial papers taking telegraph news rose from 144 to 365. That is a real widening of the telegraph-fed provincial press. It is not, on these pages, a boom created by the press rate alone: the Press Association was founded in November 1865, under the private companies, and the pages do not separate the press rate from other changes in the newspaper business. The rate was large enough to cost the Post Office £375,000 in 1900, and in 1890 the Post Office carried almost 1.5 million railway messages for nothing.

QUESTION: B8
ANSWER: Steinwender, American Economic Review 108, no. 3 (March 2018), uses the 28 July 1866 cable as a break in information delay, from about ten days by mail steamer to about one day. The published abstract: the average and the volatility of the transatlantic cotton price difference fell, average trade flows rose and became more volatile, and the efficiency gain equals 8 percent of export value. Her VoxEU summary of that paper: the variance of the New York–Liverpool price difference fell by more than 90 percent, the average difference fell by a third, and the welfare gain is about 8 percent of annual trade value. The May 2014 working paper, a draft rather than the printed article, is more numeric: average gap from 2.56 pence per pound (16 percent of the New York price) to 1.65 pence per pound, a fall of 35 percent; variance of the gap down 93 percent; average daily exports from New York to Liverpool from 460 bales up by 170 bales, or 37 percent; variance of exports up 114 percent; and a second welfare expression, equivalent to removing a tariff of about 6 percent ad valorem. The mechanism is forecasting. Exporters had to ship before Liverpool demand was known. Faster news raised exports in high-demand spells and cut the spells in which Liverpool ran short. Storage could not do this fully, because a stock cannot be negative. The result is identified off the opening of the cable, not off the later fall in the word rate.

PART C

1. The capacity later users enjoyed was not, in the main, a write-down. Western Union’s average toll fell from $1.09 in 1867 to 30 cents in 1900 while 30 to 40 cents of each revenue dollar stayed profit. The successful Atlantic cables were financed with new money in 1866, £600,000 at Anglo-American, and Cookson records them as exceptionally profitable. The British state paid twenty years’ profits, and the estimate trebled before the transfer. The second claimant was paid more than the first business plan, and then the state priced the public telegram below what that purchase could earn. The only clean write-off is the 1858 cable.
SOURCE: https://eh.net/encyclopedia/history-of-the-u-s-telegraph-industry/
QUOTE: "roughly 30 to 40 cents of every dollar of revenue were net profit for the company."

2. The cotton result is an effect of speed at the opening tariff, not of a price collapse. The 8 percent of export value and the closing of the Liverpool–New York gap are measured in the first year, when the message still cost £1 a word. Cutting the delay from ten days to one day changed shipments. The later fall to 1 shilling a word is a different episode, driven by new cables and the 1880s price war.
SOURCE: https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/how-information-frictions-affect-trade-and-price-patterns
QUOTE: "The welfare gains from the telegraph are around 8% of annual trade value."

3. Because the ocean word stayed expensive, a side business grew up to economise words. Code books and “packers” compressed a commercial paragraph into one or two chargeable words. The International Telegraph Union spent the 1870s defining what a word was, because the companies treated packing as a raid on revenue. Müller’s conclusion, in Henniker Heaton’s phrase, is that global cable communication remained beyond the means of 99 percent of the population. The social telegram people expected did not appear. The users who prospered were merchants, governments, and news agencies, plus the code-book trade that existed only while the word was dear.
SOURCE: https://d-nb.info/1288583958/34
QUOTE: "beyond the means of 99 percent of the population."

4. The largest omitted number is the railway counterfactual. Not having to double-track the United States was put at $957 million in 1893, against a Western Union book capitalization of $123 million. It is easy to miss because the railway looks like a partner and a landlord of the poles. On the British side the same relationship was written into statute: the companies kept train wires, and the Post Office paid for a perpetual wayleave. In 1890 the Post Office still carried almost 1.5 million railway messages without payment.
SOURCE: https://eh.net/encyclopedia/history-of-the-u-s-telegraph-industry/
QUOTE: "Western Union’s book capitalization was $123 million in 1893, making this seem like a good investment."

5. Two uses that the “news and markets” story under-weights, and one institutional fact that makes the British cheapness look like a technology crash when it was a tax. Swift and Armour each spent about $200,000 a year on telegrams to keep refrigerated meat moving; the scarce piece was the cold car, dated 1874, and the eastbound freight was about half the cost of shipping the animal alive. Tramp owners used the cable to cut time in port and to send ships farther for cargo, a margin usually credited to freight rates and tariffs. And the British sixpenny and press tariffs were chosen. Fawcett, as Postmaster General, treated telegraph revenue as a tax on the public rather than as a profit to be defended, and the 1885 cut dropped the average from 1s. 1d. to 8d. The press rate had been in the statute since 1868. The cheap inland message in Britain is a political price, funded by a purchase that did not pay, not residue from a bankrupt cable.
