QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: What was built was manufacturing capacity, not a civil-engineering network. From January 1895 to June 1897, Birch’s manual records 671 cycle, tube, tyre and motor corporations with £43.033 million of nominal capital. That is not cash raised. The Economic History Review manuscript records successful promotion of £17.3 million of cycle firms in 1896 and £7.4 million in 1897. Another Quinn statement puts 1896 subscribed capital of cycle, tube and tyre companies at over £17 million, about one eighth of all UK issues that year, plus £7.7 million in 1897. Between January 1896 and June 1897, 601 new cycle corporations were established. Listed cycle, tube or tyre companies went from under 10 at the end of 1895 to 127 in the summer of 1897 and 65 at the end of 1898. Aggregate market capitalisation of listed cycle firms went from about £2.5 million in 1895 to £20.8 million in April 1897 and £5.4 million in January 1899. At the 1896 peak, about 750,000 bicycles a year were produced in Britain, with about 1.5 million cyclists in a population of about 35 million. The Times put the value of that output at £11–12 million, which is £14.67–£16 per bicycle if spread evenly over 750,000 machines. In Coventry, Premier claimed 33,000 machines for 1896 and 40,000 for 1897; Swift claimed about 700 a week and nearly 1,000 workmen in 1896. About 4,000 of roughly 8,300 British cycle workers in 1891 were in Coventry. In 1906 the city made 310,000 cycles; Rudge-Whitworth made about 75,000 with 1,800 workpeople on the county-history count. British cycle, tyre and velocipede patents in 1896 were 4,269, 14.8 per cent of patents issued that year. United States output was about 30,000 bicycles in 1890, over 400,000 in 1896, and more than 900,000 in each of 1898 and 1899; a scholarly reassessment puts 1897 production at 1–2 million machines, about 1 per cent of US GNP, with about 5 million bicycles in use in 1900. Metered input: pounds sterling per bicycle in Britain, and dollars per bicycle in the United States. P*: Dunlop, floated in May 1896 at £5 million after the Pneumatic Tyre Company (nominal capital £300,000) was bought for £3 million, promised an 8 per cent ordinary dividend; cycle preference shares commonly promised 5–7 per cent. Average next dividend of cycle firms peaked at 18 per cent of par in May 1896. At an 8 per cent expected return, May 1896 prices implied dividend growth 89 percentage points above the growth observed over the next two years. Beeston’s announced 100 per cent dividend was a promoter’s payment. Price path: from summer 1897 firms cut prices. In July 1897 Rudge-Whitworth cut its Special by 44 per cent and its Standard by 37 per cent. Average dividends were 2–3 per cent of par in 1898 and 1.4 per cent in 1899. Export value fell from £1,855,614 in 1896 to £529,653 in 1900. In the United States the Wrights’ Van Cleve fell from 60–65 dollars in 1896 to 32–47 dollars in 1900; the St. Clair fell from 42.50 dollars to 30 dollars by 1899. US sales were 250,000 bicycles in 1904, a 75 per cent drop from the 1898–99 level. A trade retrospective puts an excellent English bicycle at about £8 8s in 1914. Idle capacity is not measured in floor area. Proxies: dealers ordered about three times expected sales and then cancelled; Raleigh could fill only 25 per cent of 1896 orders, built a factory, and held surplus stock in 1897; listings fell from 127 to 65; Birmingham lost 54 per cent of its 1896 cycle companies by 1900.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: Control passed by shareholder write-down, reconstruction, bankruptcy, voluntary winding-up, and a few mergers. It did not pass by nationalisation or mandated access. Of 141 listed companies, 43 went bankrupt at an average final price of 0.039 of par, 32 wound up voluntarily at 0.441 of par, 27 were reconstructed at 0.416 of par, and 17 merged at an average 2.758 of par. The merger premium is a boom-time seller’s price. 55 companies had wound up before 1900 and 58 more in 1900–10. The 21 still alive after 1910 traded at 42.2 per cent of par in December 1903. A trade retrospective says many floated concerns cancelled half the value of their shares. Named reconstructions: Swift in 1901 at 0.11 of par, Singer in 1903 at 0.05, Humber Cycle in 1900 at 0.09, Raleigh in 1899 at 0.05, then a private purchase of Raleigh in 1908. In the sample of investors, directors’ share of capital fell from 25.7 per cent before the peak to 18.7 per cent after it; informed holders sold to later buyers, especially gentlemen living near a stock exchange. In the United States the American Bicycle Company in 1899 combined about 50 makers, at least 60 per cent of output, using cash and securities. By 1904 its profit had gone from 3 million dollars a year to zero and the securities were nearly worthless. Pope then recapitalised the remains. Hendee bought his insolvent firm’s tools at auction in 1897–98. Second claim: the shareholders who held through the crash, and the reconstructed or auction buyers who took the assets at a fraction of par. First claim: the 1896–97 promoters and subscribing owners, including Hooley on Dunlop and Beeston.
SOURCE: https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/155866385/Technological_Revolutions_and_Speculative_Finance_Author_Archive.pdf
QUOTE: "On average, the 21 companies which survived beyond 1910 were trading at just 42.2 per cent of par in December 1903"
UNCERTAIN: Final share prices are not liquidators’ cash prices for buildings and tools. BT31 sale accounts would give the fraction of build cost actually paid.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Almost none of the build was long-lived civil substrate. There were no rights-of-way and no buried network. The durable pieces were factory buildings, machine tools, jigs, and patents. The perishable pieces were finished bicycles, tyres, stock, and prospectus goodwill. Dunlop’s intangibles were £4.26 million of £5.24 million of assets, about 81 per cent. What survived into the motor take-off: the diamond frame, pneumatic-tyre practice, bearing and tube methods, fitting skill, and the works of firms that stayed in business. Coventry cycle employment was still about 5,800 in 1911. About 70 per cent of Coventry cycle names listed in 1896 were gone by 1912, so most small workshops did not survive as addresses. Daimler’s important early building was a cotton mill, not a cycle shed. Early US and British water-bound macadam associated with the road agitation was damaged by cars and trucks in the mid-1910s, so it was not a durable platform for motor traffic.
SOURCE: https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.tra.013.html
QUOTE: "During the mid-1910s, automobiles severely damaged the water-bound macadam roads."

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: Inside the trade, the losers were subscribers who held, especially active gentlemen near the Birmingham and London exchanges; 43 bankrupt listed firms; tube promotions such as Accles, bankrupt in 1899 at 3 per cent of par, and Tubes Limited, reconstructed by 1906 at 1 per cent of par; and about 70 per cent of the Coventry cycle names of 1896. In the United States the losers were the American Bicycle Company’s security holders and Pope’s later car brands. Fashionable riders, whose rush in 1895–96 had filled the order books, gave the machines up when the craze passed. Ribbon weaving and watchmaking in Coventry had already lost their place to cycles before the crash: cycles replaced those trades during the rise, and the crash did not do that displacement. Horse-using interests had opposed cyclists on the roads in the 1880s; no fetched page quantifies horses displaced by cheap safeties. After 1900 the pedal-cycle departments themselves lost ground inside Coventry firms: Rover stopped cycles in 1912, and by the mid-1930s the city had ceased to be the centre of volume cycle production.

QUOTE: "only about 30 per cent. of the firms listed in 1896 were still in existence in 1912."

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: The residue was partly a cross-market input and partly more bicycle capacity. Components, tools and fitting skill were used by motor firms. The bicycle factories themselves mostly stayed with the same owners, were reconstructed, or disappeared. They were not, as a class, sold cheap to outsiders in a new industry. Swift, the 1896 reflotation of the Coventry Machinists, was reconstructed in 1901 at 11 per cent of par and made cars from 1899. Singer was reconstructed in 1903 at 5 per cent of par and sold a motor tricar in 1906. Rover, at 6 per cent of par at the end of 1903, made its first car in 1904 after financial difficulty. Humber Cycle was reconstructed in 1900 at 9 per cent of par and continued light cars in Coventry. Centaur was liquidated in 1910 and taken over by Humber in 1911. Raleigh was reconstructed in 1899 at 5 per cent of par and stayed a bicycle firm. The contrary cases are as important. Daimler, floated in 1896 with £100,000 of capital, of which Lawson took £40,000 as the patent licence, was installed in the disused cotton mills of the Coventry Spinning and Weaving Company, offered on favourable terms after a search in Cheltenham and Birmingham. Accles, a tube company of 1896, was bankrupt in 1899 at 3 per cent of par. In Springfield, Massachusetts, Hendee’s 1897 insolvency ended in an auction purchase of tools, which is the clearest cheap change of hands fetched, and it was the old owner buying them back.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: British 1896 output of about 750,000 bicycles was valued by The Times at £11–12 million, or about £14.67–£16 per bicycle on a straight division. In July 1897 Rudge-Whitworth cut the Special 44 per cent and the Standard 37 per cent. A later trade retrospective says an excellent English bicycle cost about £8 8s in 1914, which is outside the 1905 window. United States: Van Cleve 60–65 dollars in 1896 and 32–47 dollars in 1900; St. Clair 42.50 dollars falling to 30 dollars by 1899. About 5,000 people rode daily to work in downtown Chicago during the boom. No fetched page states a commuting radius in miles. Bayliss-Thomas already had a Post Office tricycle contract in the early 1880s, and tradesmen used carrier cycles for deliveries from then. Fashionable women were the 1895–96 demand shock; a trade account says society then tired of the craze and gave the bicycle up. The cheap machine widened use, and in the United States the post-crash market became largely a children’s market at lower prices. British makers were slow to offer the cheap models, which is one reason Harrison, via Quinn, gives for the industry’s loss of export value from £1,855,614 in 1896 to £529,653 in 1900.

PART C

1. The economically large “bicycle firm becomes a car firm” story is mostly the same owners redeploying a living works, which is a diffusion of an industry that already existed at P*. The landmark new car plant in Coventry was not a distressed cycle factory. Daimler in 1896 took a cotton mill.
SOURCE: https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/warks/vol8/pp162-189
QUOTE: "disbelief was expressed that there was ‘any motor car industry, properly so-called, carried on at Coventry for other than company promoting reasons’."

2. Dunlop is easy to misread as cheap tyre capacity left by the crash. The 1896 transaction capitalised patents and goodwill upward, to £5 million, and intangibles dominated the balance sheet. Shareholders of that float later sat on a 1903 price of 30 per cent of par. Motorists did not inherit a pile of written-down tyre plant at a fraction of P*.
SOURCE: https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/155866385/Technological_Revolutions_and_Speculative_Finance_Author_Archive.pdf
QUOTE: "this category accounted for £4.26 million of its £5.24 million assets."

3. Weldless-tube capacity, often listed as the gift to motor and aircraft frames, was among the worst financial wreckage. Accles was bankrupt in 1899 at a final price of 0.03 of par. Tubes Limited was reconstructed in 1906 at 0.01 of par. The tube design survived inside other firms. The promoted tube companies largely did not.
SOURCE: https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/155866385/Technological_Revolutions_and_Speculative_Finance_Author_Archive.pdf
QUOTE: "Accles | 1896 | 1.00 | 1899 | 0.03 | Bankruptcy"

4. The chainless bicycle failed as a product and left a machine-tool lineage. Leland and Faulconer’s bevel-gear plant, built for that bicycle, was cutting Cadillac rear-axle gears by 1902. Baldwin Cycle Chain Company of Worcester in 1900 turned deliberately to automobile chains. The non-obvious residue was gear-cutting and chain plant, not unused bicycles.
SOURCE: https://thebhc.org/sites/default/files/beh/BEHprint/v005/p0051-p0075.pdf
QUOTE: "the Baldwin Cycle Chain Company of Worcester, Massachusetts, which began in 1900 paying special attention to the manufacture of a line of chains suitable for automobiles"

5. Two causal arrows run the other way, and one popular residue is thin. Deere and Webber sold bicycles in the mid-1890s from an agricultural-implement base and dropped the planned Chicago bicycle plant when demand fell, so farm-implement stamping fed the bicycle boom and then exited. Fashionable women were a cause of the 1895–96 orders, and their exit helped start the slump. The Good Roads campaign began in 1888 and secured a 10,000-dollar federal inquiry in 1893; Hugill finds automobilists the stronger lobby after 1900, and finds that cars and trucks destroyed the water-bound macadam. Cyclists left a lobby and a set of maps. They did not leave a cheap paved network.
