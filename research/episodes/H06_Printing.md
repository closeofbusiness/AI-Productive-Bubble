Part B

QUESTION: B1
ANSWER: Two capacities were built, and they should not be treated as one boom. In the fifteenth century the capacity was movable-type shops. Dittmar’s draft, using the Incunabula Short Title Catalogue, counts 27,873 editions before 1501 and about 205 cities with a press by 1500, roughly one in ten European cities in his city set. Urs Leu counts printing at 16 cities in 1470, 81 in 1480, 98 in 1490, and 75 in 1500, and 261 locations at some point in the century. Venice alone accounts for 3,705 incunabula editions and, over the century, some 200 firms; Italy produced 36.4 percent of incunabula editions. A complete font in the mid-sixteenth century cost the equivalent of 4 to 10 years of a craftsman’s wages, so the fixed outlay was large relative to a shop. The metered input is the price of a printed book, or of a printed sheet, relative to a manuscript book. P* was not a published tariff. For Gutenberg it was repayment of Fust’s advances: a claim of 2,026 gulden, against loans of 1,550 guilders said to have been borrowed at 6 percent, secured on the equipment. For a Roman or Venetian edition it was the revenue needed to clear paper, wages, and type on a run of a few hundred copies. Sweynheym and Pannartz typically printed 275 to 300 copies and by March 1472 had 12,475 volumes, many unsold. The price path: at least a 65 percent fall in book prices from 1450 to 1500 (Dittmar draft), and a cut of two-thirds or more within a generation (Buringh and van Zanden). Rubin, citing Spitz and Buringh and van Zanden, says a fall of around 85 percent; Buringh and van Zanden’s own sentence is “two-thirds or more,” while their long-run quantity decomposition implies a larger cumulative fall by 1700–1800. No source fetched gives the share of press-hours left idle. What sat idle was inventory: unbound, unsold books. In the nineteenth century the relevant machine was a steam cylinder press from 1814, at 1,100 sheets an hour on The Times, and a hand press at about 250 sheets an hour. Augustus Applegath and Edward Cowper’s flat-bed machines for The Times in 1828 exceeded 4,000 sheets an hour. Hoe’s rotary press is a product of 1843–47. The metered input for readers is the price per newspaper copy. The metered input for advertisers is the price of reaching one reader. There is no prospectus P* for an 1820s rotary-press flotation, because that market did not exist. The Times’s own target was a faster impression of the paper it already sold. Idle rotary capacity in the 1820s was zero because the machines had not been built.

QUESTION: B3
ANSWER: Control changed by lawsuit, exit, and a creditors’ trust over copyrights. It did not change by nationalisation or by a distressed sale of a press network. In 1455 the second claim on Gutenberg’s project was the unfavorable repayment judgment. Fust claimed 2,026 gulden. The instrument does not record a final dissolution, and it is unclear whether the press changed hands. In Rome, Sweynheym and Pannartz dissolved the partnership after May 1473. The pope granted a few benefices in Mainz. That did not buy the unsold stock, and no fraction of cost recovered is stated. In Venice, shops exited after the 1472 glut, and the survivors, including the houses around Aldus Manutius and Andrea Torresani, accumulated privileges. On 1 August 1517 the Senate cancelled existing privileges, opened those texts to any printer, and allowed new privileges only for new works, by a two-thirds vote of the Senate. That was a regulatory write-down of monopoly rights, not a sale of machines. In 1826 the failed assets were bills and copyrights. Constable and Co. stopped on 14 January 1826. Scott owed £121,000 and refused a trade bankruptcy that could have paid as little as 7 shillings in the pound. Creditors accepted a private trust over his future literary earnings, with a £20,000 life policy payable to the trust. Constable was left an undischarged bankrupt. Robert Cadell kept the author. The Magnum Opus, 48 volumes issued by Cadell from 1829 to 1833, was the salvage: a cheaper collected edition of novels already written. The debt was £53,000 at Scott’s death on 21 September 1832 and was cleared in 1847 by the sale of the remaining copyrights. No fetched page states the price Cadell paid as a fraction of the original printing-house cost, because the written-down asset was not the presses.

QUESTION: B4
ANSWER: Almost all of the physical build was short-lived equipment and consumable paper, not a long-lived substrate. A shop’s capital was the press, a font of type, wages, and paper. Type wore out. Paper became the product or the unsold stock. There were no earthworks, rights-of-way, or buried cables. What survived into the beneficiaries’ period was the craft, the fonts and presses that could be moved or resold, the Frankfurt fair as a trading calendar, and a distribution habit. Urs Leu notes that large editions needed storage, which smaller firms often could not afford. In the nineteenth century The Times’s 1814 machines were used, not abandoned. Applegath and Cowper’s 1828 machines were also used, and they are what made a 200,000-copy weekly physically possible. Hoe’s rotary presses were built in the late 1840s for newspapers that were already large. The 1826 residue that actually sat on a balance sheet and was revalued was intangible: Scott’s copyrights and the duplicated bills. The Magnum Opus of 1829–33 is the reuse of that intangible, in the same market of novel-readers.
SOURCE: https://blog.nationalmuseum.ch/en/2017/05/book-printing-in-europe-3/
QUOTE: "having invested heavily in purchases of printing presses, composers’ utensils, and paper, in addition to wages"
UNCERTAIN: No fetched page gives a percentage split of printing capital between buildings and machines. A probate inventory of a Venetian shop would settle the share.

QUESTION: B5
ANSWER: The displaced parties were copyists, overextended printers, and, later, the subscription newspaper’s exclusive hold on the urban reader. Manuscript production in central Europe fell after 1470 as printing spread. Sweynheym and Pannartz, who had flooded Rome with classical and patristic editions, dissolved in 1473. In Venice, printers who missed the privilege race went bankrupt or turned to reprints; Cicero’s De officiis is the type of the duplicated title, with 28 editions by 1482. The industry’s own response was to rebuild scarcity: the 1517 decree stripped old privileges and confined new ones to new books. On 29 November 1814 The Times displaced its hand pressmen. Walter offered full wages until they found other work, after installing the machines in secret. In New York the loser was the 6-cent, 10-dollar-a-year paper as the only way to read a daily. The buyer’s price fell to 1 cent, and the share of New Yorkers buying a daily rose from about 1 in 16 in 1830 to about 1 in 4 in 1850. In Britain the loser on the legal market was the unstamped radical sheet once the stamp fell. In 1836 the Chancellor of the Exchequer told the Commons that stamped English newspapers used 36 million stamps in twelve months, while one seized unstamped paper already had 40,000 copies of an unfinished issue, a rate he put at upwards of 2 million copies a year, about one-eighteenth of the stamped total. The Times itself was not displaced. It owned the fast presses and remained a high-priced paper because the stamp, not the machine, set the floor.

QUOTE: "By 1476 no less than seventeen editions, and by 1482 twenty-eight editions of the work had come on the market."

QUESTION: B6
ANSWER: For books, the metered input is the price of a printed book relative to a manuscript. Between 1450 and 1500 that price fell by at least 65 percent. Within a generation the cost of books was cut by two-thirds or more. Over the long window from 1450/1500 to 1700/1800, falling prices account for a tenfold rise in per capita book production, against a slightly less than threefold rise from literacy. A single pre-1470 and post-1470 price for one standard title, in gulden per copy, was not on the fetched pages. For newspapers, the metered input is the price per copy. In New York in 1830 a daily cost 6 cents, and proprietors preferred a subscription of about 10 dollars a year, about one week’s wages for a skilled journeyman. On 3 September 1833 The Sun sold at 1 cent a copy. In London in 1815 The Times cost 6 pence a copy, of which 4 pence was newspaper stamp duty, despite the steam press having been in use since 29 November 1814. On 20 June 1836 the Chancellor of the Exchequer carried a resolution to cut the stamp from 4 pence to 1 penny per sheet. Advertisement duty was repealed in 1853, newspaper stamp duty in 1855, and paper duty in 1861. The British copy price therefore fell first because the tax fell, not because a rotary press had been written down.

QUESTION: B7
ANSWER: The Sun’s model, stated in the first issue, was a paper “within the means of every one” that would also be “an advantageous medium for advertising.” The advertisers were a different customer from the old subscriber. The fetched evidence shows the existence of that customer. It does not name A. T. Stewart as one of them, and it does not itemise patent-medicine linage in the Sun. The named firm whose books survive is British. Thomas Holloway, from 1837–38, bought newspaper space as the distribution system for a pill and an ointment: £5,000 in 1842, rising to about £50,000 a year by his death in 1883, with profits of about £50,000 a year. His advertisements ran in papers worldwide, with directions translated into many languages. That business works only when the cost of reaching a buyer is low. A 6-cent or 6-pence paper, sold to a small subscribed public, cannot support an advertising budget of that size relative to a cheap repeated purchase. Classified and local advertising on the Sun is attested by the paper’s own purpose and by the street-sale system. The trades behind those small advertisements are the employers and shopkeepers who had something to fill today. They are economically broader than Holloway, and less famous, because no single brand captured the gain.

UNCERTAIN: The share of Sun advertising revenue from patent medicines, department stores, and help-wanted notices in the 1830s. A classified count from the Sun and the Herald for 1833–40 would settle B7’s American half.

Part C

1. The asset written down in 1826 was literary copyright and a web of accommodation bills, and the salvage was a cheaper edition of the same novels. Scott owed £121,000. Creditors took a trust over future books instead of 7 shillings in the pound. Cadell’s Magnum Opus, 48 volumes from 1829 to 1833, sold the Waverley novels to a wider but still ordinary novel-reading public. That is consolidation and repricing inside publishing. It does not create a downstream industry. Contemporaries saw a famous author ruined by his publisher’s bills, which hid the fact that nothing like a press network had been built or abandoned.
SOURCE: https://www.walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk/biography/finance.html
QUOTE: "a readily affordable edition of the Waverley Novels with new introductions and copious notes."

2. The penny paper was not a use of idle steam capacity left by an 1820s crash. Day printed the first thousand copies of The Sun by hand, and the work took him all day. Steam came in 1835, after daily sales had reached 19,000. In Britain the floor under the copy price was the stamp. The Times already had steam printing in 1814 and in 1815 still sold at 6 pence, of which 4 pence was duty. The Commons cut that duty from 4 pence to 1 penny only on 20 June 1836. A history that starts from rotary-press speculation misses both the sequence and the tax.
SOURCE: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1836/jun/20/stamp-duty-on-newspapers
QUOTE: "the duty on newspapers should be reduced from 4 d., less the discount, to 1 d."

3. Cheap print was refused where the valuable complement was a secret. The Dutch East India Company, chartered in 1602, used hand-drawn charts on vellum for the routes east of South Africa for about 150 years, because those routes were the company’s advantage. A printed atlas for the voyage from the Netherlands to the East Indies appeared in 1753 as part of Johannes van Keulen II’s Zee-Fakkel. It was issued only to the company’s captains, for one voyage, and had to be returned. It was not sold. Public printed pilots served European coasts. The richest long-distance trade kept the chart scarce on purpose. From the deck of a 1470s print shop, or of a nineteenth-century newspaper, that split was not the obvious use of a cheaper page.
SOURCE: https://www.theheritageportal.co.za/article/secret-sea-chart-revealed
QUOTE: "For 150 years the VOC produced only manuscript charts: hand-drawn on vellum"

4. Some of the knowledge that became cheap had been a deliberate trade secret, so the people who held it had a reason not to welcome the press. Compound-interest tables circulated in manuscript among merchants and were “kept hidden by those who had them as great secrets.” Simon Stevin’s Tafelen van interest was printed in 1582, with an enlarged Amsterdam edition in 1590, once the city’s trade made a public manual saleable. The scarce complement was destroyed by publication. The gain went to ordinary merchants who could now compute interest without buying a secret. This is a third-claim mechanism that looks, from the outside, like just another arithmetic book.
SOURCE: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/4296021/61245_Lesger_Amsterdam_information2.pdf
QUOTE: "kept hidden by those who had them as great secrets"

5. After the glut, the printing trade tried to put scarcity back. The Venetian decree of 1 August 1517 said privileges had become so numerous that printers were leaving the city. It threw already printed works into the public domain and allowed a new privilege only for a new work. That is the opposite of a gift of permanently cheap capacity to outsiders. It is the survivors rewriting property rights so that a book could be expensive again. Combined with Buringh and van Zanden’s result that falling prices, not rising literacy, did most of the work of raising book consumption, the draft sentence about infrastructure “fueling mass literacy” points at the wrong variable and the wrong decade.
