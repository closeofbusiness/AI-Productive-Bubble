## 0. The question, and the vocabulary

**The project owner's goal:** find the businesses that prosper from the AI build-out **without being part of it**. That
excludes chips, IT, data centres, and the firms that build or run them. The method is history: for each past
bubble, map what it left **abundant and cheap**, and who used that as a core input to their own business. Then
extrapolate to AI.

**Vocabulary** (from the market-plumbing *Third_Derivative_Concept_Map*, Input-Price Frame):

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Metered input** | The one capacity whose unit price fell, with a unit: freight per ton-mile, $/Mbps-month, ¢/kWh, $/GPU-hour. |
| **P\*** | The price the builders' own plans needed in order to pay. |
| **First claim** | The builder, who financed at P\*. |
| **Second claim** | Whoever took the write-down: shareholders, bondholders, receivers, distressed buyers, the state. |
| **Third claim** ("third derivative", strict) | A business that works **only because** the price fell below P\*, and that sells to a **different customer set** from the builders'. |
| **Diffusion winner** (the project owner's broader sense) | Grew on the new infrastructure, but would have grown at P\* too. |
| **Scarce complement** | What the winner held that stayed scarce after the input became cheap. Value goes there (Teece 1986). |

Both kinds of winner are mapped below and kept apart. Historically the diffusion winners are much bigger.
Third claims are where the non-obvious opportunities are.

## 1. Headline findings

1. **Strict third claims are specific and usually small. Diffusion winners are everywhere.** Of the 13 historic
   episodes, 10 have at least one clean third claim, usually small next to the diffusion winners. Three have
   none: canals, steamships, the Insull crash (no third claim from the crash itself; Insull's cheap-power third claims came through R4, see H09 in §2). Of the 9 further episodes scouted (H15), 4 produced third claims: shale gas (including Sabine Pass), battery
   cells, solar modules, crypto mining.
   Every third claim sells to a customer group **missing from the builders' business case**: freight and milk on a passenger railway, video viewers and
   physicists on carriers' fibre, paint makers on kerosene refineries, daytime soap sponsors on an evening ad
   market, Pakistani farmers on panels made for solar developers.
2. **How the input got cheap decides whether anything new forms** (§4, L1). Five routes:
   - **write-down *with* transfer of control**: fibre, Standard Oil's Cleveland refineries, Core Scientific. Third claims appear fast, in 0–5 years.
   - **write-down *without* transfer**: UK and US railways, rubber estates. Mostly consolidation.
   - **cheaper new vintages (technological deflation)**: steamships, bicycles, DRAM, and **AI accelerators and tokens**. Broad diffusion, no durable cost advantage.
   - **statutory or contracted prices**: the 1868 telegraph press rate, Niagara and Bonneville aluminium contracts.
   - **unsold inventory and by-products at disposal prices**: radio's half-price daytime hours, refinery naphtha, ethane in the gas stream.
3. **A securities collapse above a solvent operating asset passes nothing downstream.** In the Insull crash
   (1932) the average price per kWh **rose**, from 2.57¢ (1929) to 2.85¢ (1932).
4. **Leftover capacity in the builders' own market predicts consolidation, never new industries.** This held in
   every case tested: US railroads, the US telegraph, kerosene, bicycles, radio sets, tyres, merino sheep,
   mulberry trees, DRAM, US offices, supertankers. Your framework's exclusion rule had no exception in this set.
5. **The leftovers must still have demand (C2).** The mania canals, the Pennsylvania Main Line, streetcars and
   supertankers left cheap capacity that founded nothing. The Kennet & Avon toll was cut to ½d per ton-mile and
   drew no new traffic ✅.
6. **What transfers is the long-lived substrate. The overlay is replaced.** Railway earthworks, not the iron rails
   (about 3.5 years' life ✅). Conduit and glass, not the optics, which were at least ⅔ of lit cost ✅. Cleared
   pasture, not the sheep. **The energised megawatt, not the mining chips** (Core Scientific → CoreWeave ✅).
7. **The winner holds the complement that stays scarce**, not the cheap asset: audience and licences (YouTube,
   Netflix), cold chain (Swift, Vestey, United Fruit), brand and patent (Vaseline, Durex), the power contract and
   site (Alcoa), the permitted coastal site (Cheniere). Operators of the written-down asset earn utility returns.
8. **The synthesis we started from has many errors.** The corrections table (§3) has 20 rows, among them "steel"
   rails in the 1840s, the bicycle crash "in 1901", "RCA invented radio advertising", oil "just in time for the
   combustion engine", tulips as the control group, and "dot-com was constrained by the speed of laying cable".
9. **For AI, two questions, two answers.** *Enablement* (the project owner's question, `03_AI_Enablement_Map.md` v1.5): with only
   general-purpose AI costs counting (the Netflix-bandwidth analogy, rule d), the candidate 2023–26 enablement is narrow
   (evidence verified; new output and consumption not yet shown) —
   long-form text and speech collapse a fixed cost per title or language. Only translated fiction holds up on consumption
   (AI-translated works ~40% of WebNovel's novel revenue, H1 2026, issuer filing); auto-dubbed creator video and synthetic
   narration are weak (no RPM; .03% of reporting publishers' 2025 audiobook receipts); none STRONG (`03` v1.5). On watch: mid-list manga, short-run instruments,
   waste-heat hosts. Sector-specific AI tools that may create new output (unstaffed school sport streams, designed
   reagents, one-off machined parts) are excluded unless the project owner relaxes that rule. The physical channel
   runs in reverse (AI bids up DRAM, transformers, fibre, turbines, electricians).
   *Note, 16 Nov 2026: `05` governs this bullet. Web fiction did not pass (05 §5.6), so translated fiction is not a survivor. Rule (d) is not an exclusion: chains it removed go to a kept-and-scored second book (05 §4, §9 item 1, decided 16 Oct 2026).*
   *Strict third claims* (§6, version 2, after the red-team): as of 14 Oct 2026 **no candidate class survives as a strict third
   claim**. Of 56 classes, 20 were refuted, 35 weakened, and 1 survives: the frame claim that energised capacity is
   the durable residue.
   - **There is no bust**: demand exceeds capacity, and GPU contract prices are above break-even and rising.
   - **Cheapness runs only through R3**: tokens at fixed capability fall about 13× a year. That is the steamship
     pattern: everyone gets it at once, and the value goes to holders of scarce complements.
   - **Assets flow *into* AI** (reverse flow): idled smelter interconnections, bitcoin-mining sites and battery
     sites.
   - **Boom-phase winners** are incumbents holding complements (records, heat networks, licences, installed bases,
     market position) and heavy industry collecting capital and scarcity rents.
   - **Strict third claims are pre-registered as conditional on a bust.** The switch is dashboard signal J3.

## 4. The logic map: cross-episode regularities

### L1. Five routes to a cheap input, and only some create third claims

| Route | Mechanism | Episodes | What forms downstream | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **R1** write-down **with** transfer of control | the asset is sold at a fraction of cost to an owner with no sunk commitment | fibre (Global Crossing 1.8%, PSINet → Cogent ✅); Cleveland refineries (40–45¢ ✅); Hendee's tools; Core Scientific → CoreWeave ✅ | **third claims, fast**: video, research networks, offshore back offices, by-product industries, AI hosting | 0–5 y |
| **R2** write-down **without** transfer | the same owners keep the asset and take a lower return | UK railways (shares –64%), US railroads (coupons –35% ✅), rubber estates, canal premiums | mostly **consolidation**; third claims only where operators price marginal traffic as fill-in (milk, car lines) | 5–25 y |
| **R3** technological deflation | each new vintage is cheaper, so P\* itself falls | steamships (cheaper hulls 25–35% ✅), bicycles, DRAM, cells, **GPUs, tokens** | **broad diffusion**, no durable cost advantage; temporary gains in cycle troughs | continuous |
| **R4** statutory or contracted price | a state or a long contract sets the price below what the market would | UK press rate ✅; 1844 penny train; Niagara and Bonneville aluminium ✅; *reverse case:* the Stevenson cartel raised rubber 3.5× in 1925 | third claims created by the price-setter, and **revocable** | policy-timed |
| **R5** unsold inventory or by-products | joint products or off-peak capacity priced at disposal value | radio daytime at ½ card ✅; naphtha, gasolene and still residue ✅; ethane in the gas stream ✅ | **third claims with no crash at all** | 1–20 y |
| *(R0)* securities-only collapse | the paper above a solvent asset is written off | Insull (kWh price rose) | **nothing passes downstream** | — |

### L2. The third claim is a customer the business case never priced

Every clean third claim sells to a customer class absent from the builders' prospectus:

| Built for | Unpriced customer that showed up |
|---|---|
| passenger railway | freight shippers; dairymen |
| carriers' wholesale capacity | video viewers, physicists, foreign back offices |
| lamp oil | painters, building owners off the gas mains, chemists |
| evening sponsors | housewives' daytime, sold to soap makers |
| carmakers (battery cells) | grid storage |
| utility-scale solar developers | Pakistani farmers |
| an import terminal | foreign LNG buyers |

The builders' own customers, served more cheaply, are diffusion. **Early warning:** contemporaries say they are
surprised by the traffic mix. In 1850 there was surprise at freight; on the Great Western the milk trade was
"ridiculed" in 1860.

### L3. The threshold rule: when does cheapness matter?

A third claim forms where the cheap input is a **large share of the user's unit cost**, use is **continuous or
bulk**, and the product is **low in value per unit**. Examples: milk by the gallon, two hours a day of 1 Mbps
video (≈$100/month at 1998 transit vs ≈$2 at 2007 transit), an offshore voice trunk, ethane as cracker
feedstock, smelting at 1–2 mills/kWh. Where the input is a small line item, the user is diffusion: SaaS
(bandwidth was under 1% of cost), Indian software exports, Skype, high-value oranges and reapers.

### L4. Perishability and speed recur

The same story keeps appearing in different goods: milk, dressed beef, frozen mutton, bananas, news, cotton
prices, storm warnings. The scarce piece is usually the **cold chain or the time-critical network**, not the
cheap haul.

### L5. Value goes to the scarce complement, not to the buyer of the cheap asset

This confirms Concept Map §6. Cogent and Level 3 earned utility returns. The equity value went to
Netflix (licences, ratings), YouTube (audience), Swift (branch houses and reefers), Vestey (refrigerated tonnage
plus British retail), Alcoa (the contract and the site), Cheniere (the permitted site and berths), and
CoreWeave (the customer relationship; the megawatt stayed with Core Scientific).

### L6. Substrate transfers; overlay is replaced

What later users actually used was the durable layer:

| Durable layer (transferred) | Overlay (replaced) |
|---|---|
| railway earthworks and right-of-way | iron rails, ~3.5 years ✅ |
| conduit and glass | lighting electronics, ≥⅔ of lit cost ✅ |
| Suez Canal, harbours | ship hulls |
| cleared hill pasture | the sheep |
| tanks and berths (Sabine Pass) | — |
| powered shells and interconnection (Core Scientific) | mining ASICs, shipped to other mines |

The third claim rides the old substrate with **new** overlay. This is your C4.

### L7. Leftover capacity in the builders' own market → consolidation (your exclusion rule: no exception in 11 cases tested)

Western Union, Standard Oil, the US rail "communities of interest", BSA/Raleigh/Hercules, the tyre Big Four,
radio-set makers, DRAM. A sub-pattern: the lasting firm can be the one that *exits* the glut product and keeps
the scarce complement. Intel left DRAM and kept the microprocessor; Cheney left mulberries for imported silk.

### L8. The leftovers need surviving demand (C2)

Wrong-network cases founded nothing: the Kennet & Avon toll at ½d ✅, the Pennsylvania Main Line, streetcars,
supertankers used as floating storage (dearer than tanks on land), US offices.

### L9. Three special shapes worth hunting

- **A new form of the input.** Liquid latex let dipped and foamed goods exist; wild rubber smoked on the river
  could not.
- **Reversal.** An import terminal became an export terminal (Sabine Pass ✅); railways bought canals to close them.
- **Bypassing the one input that stays expensive.** Disc syndication went around AT&T's lines; Cogent sold
  $10/Mbps unmetered on bankrupt PSINet ✅; Google bought dark fibre instead of buying transport.

### L10. Industries inside the new chain, and institutions after the crash

- **Inside the chain:** manufactured ice, packer by-products, private car lines, machine-tool makers, code books.
  The code-book trade existed *because* cable words stayed expensive.
- **After the crash:** the audit profession (Deloitte and the Great Western, 1849), PUHCA and the SEC, ratings
  services.

By the strict test these are builder-side services. They are real, non-obvious opportunity classes all the same.

### L11. Lags carry no signal (consistent with Concept Map §9)

R1 transfers: 0–5 years. R2 and R5: 7–25 years (the L1 table gives R2 as 5–25 y; both ranges stand, and neither is a screen, 05 §3.1). Social diffusion: 40–55 years (football specials). Lag must not
be used as a screen.

### L12. Foresight sat with outsiders, not builders

- Swift's own partner called the scheme unsound.
- The trunk lines refused Swift's cars; the Grand Trunk, with no livestock investment, took them.
- Netflix's February 2007 10-K said streaming would surpass DVD only "at some point".
- Cheniere's CEO pivoted to export by 2010.
- Universities took Level 3's dark fibre within a year of the bankruptcies.

The builder's existing book is what blinds it. That is your C9, shared-asset incompatibility.

### L13. Cheap codified knowledge moves value from holders of secrets to holders of position (printing, H06)

- **The gainers.** Printed compound-interest tables, until then "kept hidden by those who had them as great
  secrets" ✅, let ordinary merchants compute interest. The Amsterdam weekly price list, 2 stuivers a copy ✅,
  gave small firms the information only large correspondence networks had held.
- **Where value went.** It stayed with whoever held **position**: the Bourse seat, the cargo, the pulpit, the
  brand (Holloway).
- **The refusals.** Where the secret *was* the business, cheap print was refused. The VOC kept its East Indies
  charts hand-drawn for 150 years.
- **The industry's response.** The printers themselves rebuilt scarcity through property rights: the Venetian
  decree of 1517 granted privileges only for new works.

**This is the closest historic analogue to cheap synthetic cognition** (see M11).

### L14. The fixed cost per unit of variety (added 15 Oct, enablement round)

Cheap **general** capability enables businesses where a fixed cost per title or per language was binding: the long tail
of an *existing* category gets made, not a new industry from zero. Historic form: AWS cut the cost of a first experiment;
first VC rounds fell ~20% and initial investments nearly doubled (Ewens, Nanda & Rhodes-Kropf). AI form: fewer than 5% of
Amazon.com titles were ever offered in more than one language; sellable (post-edited) translation now costs ~90% less
(China Literature, vendor), raw machine translation under $10 against ~£7,200–8,000 for a novel. Value goes to whoever
distributes the tail. **Count consumption, not titles**: a tail can flood (lead, music: AI tracks 28% of Deezer
deliveries with ~0.5% of streams in Sep 2025; 44% of daily uploads with ~1–3% of streams in Apr 2026). Detail: `03` §4.

### L15. Crowding: the reverse threshold (added 15 Oct)

During scarcity the anchor buyer bids away inputs it shares with others and **un-enables** product classes: DRAM (DDR4
8Gb chip $1.50 → $25, Oct 2023–Aug 2026), transformers (+77% since 2019), fibre (+70% from the 2021 trough), gas
turbines, electricians, interconnections moving from smelters into data centres. The learning curves AI rides were paid
for by EVs, shale and ADAS. Apart from by-product heat (R5), physical enablement needs an AI-anchored decline that reaches
others, or a bust (J3). For memory, the 2019 and 2023 gluts restored existing products (more memory in existing phones, a
cheaper existing Raspberry Pi) rather than enabling new ones, and suppliers see tightness through 2027–28 (`03` §6, F08).

### L16. A statute that looks like a cost fall (added 15 Oct; refines R4)

Every measured cost collapse among this round's finance, compliance and energy-sharing candidates came from a statute or
a public rail: Aadhaar e-KYC, TReDS, EMIR Art. 9(1a), SEC Rule 6c-11, Austria's 2021 energy-community law. Statutes also *push* data-centre heat reuse (Germany's EnEfG §11, lead; Denmark's executive order
of Oct 2025 and Finland's Act 1382/2025, both a duty with a cost-benefit escape) and *pull* captioning (ADA Title II; lead).
Before crediting "AI cut the cost of X", check for a concurrent rule change.

### L17. The free layer is the platform's (added 15 Oct; extends L5 and screen 10)

When a builder or distributor supplies the AI layer at zero (YouTube auto-dub, the Kindle Translate beta), the
enablement is real for the producer, but the rent stays with the platform and the layer can be withdrawn. Where a tail
floods, platforms ration attention — seen so far in music (lead); book, audiobook and video platforms use only labels and
distribution limits (Oct 2026). Consumption decides: AI-translated works earned 40% of WebNovel's novel revenue in H1 2026
(issuer filing), while AI-narrated audiobooks drew .03% of reporting publishers' 2025 audiobook receipts (APA).

## 5. The "third derivative" as an early-warning system

The project owner's definition: *acceleration of acceleration*, a way to spot inflection points before they show. In the
historic record, third claims were preceded by measurable signals. These are the ones to track.

*Note, 16 Nov 2026: 05 §3.2a governs how the third derivative is computed. It is taken per chain on trailing four-quarter log changes, and a J verdict is never copied onto a chain.*

| # | Signal | Historic instance | What it measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **J1** | The metered price falls **through P\*** and the decline accelerates | transit $1,200 → $200 → $25 (1998–2007); ethane $0.97 → $0.09 | the input crossing the threshold of a new user class |
| **J2** | An operator carries traffic it calls **unremunerative** | railway milk; the UK press rate (£375k loss in 1900); naphtha "sold at the purchaser's price" ✅ | a price below P\* that is already in use |
| **J3** | A **distressed transfer at a small fraction of build cost** | Global Crossing ~1.8%; PSINet ~$10m; Core Scientific equity –98% → 12-year lease | C1 firing; control moving to an agent with no sunk cost |
| **J4** | The **traffic mix accelerates toward a customer class missing from the prospectus** | passenger share 68% → 47% (1844–53); VoIP minutes ×15 (2001–03) | the unpriced customer arriving |
| **J5** | **Non-traditional buyers of the raw substrate** appear | universities buying dark fibre (2003); Google buying strands; INEOS designing for US ethane | the scarce-complement holders moving |
| **J6** | **The incumbent refuses** the new use | trunk lines refuse Swift's cars; AT&T keeps the wires; Netflix's 10-K hedges | C9 shared-asset incompatibility; the opening is for an outsider |

### 6.0 The verdict in five lines

1. **As of 14 October 2026, no candidate class survives as a strict third claim.** Final verdicts on 56 classes:
   20 refuted, 35 weakened, and **1 survives**. The survivor is not a business class. It is the frame claim that
   energised capacity is the durable residue.
2. **The reason is that there is no bust.**
   - Demand exceeds capacity: Microsoft, 29 Jul 2026; Alphabet raised 2026 capex to $195–205bn.
   - The B200 transaction index stood at $7.83/GPU-hour on 13 Oct, against a break-even (P\*) of $4.75, up 10.9% in 30 days.
   - Historic third claims needed cheapness through write-down with transfer, unsold inventory or by-products, or
     a statutory price (R1, R5, R4). Today the only route running is **R3**: tokens at fixed capability fall about
     **13× a year** (Epoch, 22 Sep 2026), slowing to about 4.7× once a capability level is two years old (05 §3.2 adds about 75× a year for a capability just debuted, from the same Epoch release). Every user
     gets that at once, so the value goes to whoever holds the complement. That is the steamship pattern.
3. **Assets are flowing into AI ("reverse flow").** Today AI is the *third claimant on other booms' leftovers*:
   - Century's Hawesville smelter site and interconnection went to TeraWulf (482 MW over 15 years, on the smelter's
     own contract form).
   - Bitcoin-mining sites went to CoreWeave and Microsoft: Core Scientific has about 1.1 GW leased, and IREN signed
     $9.7bn with Microsoft.
   - Eolian's Chisholm battery interconnection was lent to a CyrusOne campus.
   - Plug Power's New York electricity rights are going to a data-centre developer.
4. **Who gains now (boom phase):**
   - **(a) incumbents holding scarce complements** who adopt cheap AI: a licence, an installed base, data, a network,
     a market position. This is *diffusion*, not third claims.
   - **(b) heavy industry, through capital and scarcity rents, not cheap inputs.** Century funds smelter restarts
     from the Hawesville sale proceeds. EAF steel mills are paid for curtailability in capacity markets clearing at
     the cap.
   - **(c) the holders of heat networks** that take data-centre waste heat.
5. **Strict third claims stay conditional on a bust.** The switch to watch is dashboard signal **J3** (§6.4).
